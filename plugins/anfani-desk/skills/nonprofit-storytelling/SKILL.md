# Nonprofit Storytelling

## When to Use This Skill

Use this skill when a nonprofit needs to develop, vet, or fix narrative content that centers a
beneficiary, client, community, or program impact — the "case for support" underlying fundraising,
program, and communications work. Typical triggers:

- Writing or editing a beneficiary/client story for a newsletter, gala video script, grant narrative,
  or website "our impact" section.
- Reviewing an existing story draft for dignity violations, savior framing, or trauma exploitation
  before it goes public.
- Building or revising an organization's storytelling/ethics guidelines, consent process, or
  interview protocol.
- Training board members, program staff, or volunteers on how to talk about clients publicly.
- Structuring the overarching "case for support" narrative that other materials (appeals, proposals,
  annual reports) will draw from.

**Boundary:** This skill produces the *narrative content and ethical process* — the story itself. It
does not lay out an annual report (`nonprofit-annual-report`), define brand voice/tone/key messages
(`nonprofit-brand-messaging`), format social posts (`nonprofit-social-media`), pitch a story to a
journalist (`nonprofit-media-relations`), or write conversion-optimized donation page copy
(`nonprofit-donation-page-copy`). Those skills consume storytelling output; this skill creates it.

## Core Framework: The Dignity-Centered Narrative Arc

Use this in place of the generic "problem → solution → happy ending" arc, which is the structural
root of savior framing.

1. **Protagonist, not prop.** The beneficiary is the subject of their own story, with agency, voice,
   and a name (or chosen pseudonym) — not an illustration of the donor's or organization's virtue.
   Test: if you removed your organization's name from the story, does the person still have a full
   story? If not, rewrite.
2. **Context before crisis.** Open with who the person is (roles, strengths, community, aspirations)
   before any mention of hardship. Never lead with the worst moment of someone's life as the hook.
3. **Structural cause, not personal failing.** Name the systemic condition (housing costs, disaster,
   underfunded schools, disability access) driving the need — avoid language implying the person's
   choices or character caused their situation.
4. **Collaboration, not rescue.** The organization is a partner or resource the person used, not the
   hero who saved them. Replace "we saved Maria" with "Maria worked with our team to..."
5. **Forward agency.** Close on the person's own goals or ongoing agency, not a completed rescue
   arc — real stories rarely have a bow on top, and implying total transformation from one program
   is usually inaccurate and unsustainable as a narrative promise.
6. **Specific, sensory, non-generic detail.** One vivid, true, consented-to detail beats five
   abstractions ("case manager," "resources," "journey"). Generic language is often a sign the story
   was written *about* someone rather than *with* them.

### The "Savior Framing" Checklist (apply to every draft)

Flag and rewrite if the story contains any of these:

- Before/after language that implies the org single-handedly transformed a life ("Before us, she had
  nothing. Now she has everything.")
- Donor or staff positioned as the emotional center or hero of someone else's story.
- Poverty/suffering imagery or language used for shock value disconnected from the person's own
  framing of their experience.
- Children or vulnerable adults photographed/described in ways that emphasize helplessness
  (downcast eyes, ragged clothing framing, crying) rather than dignity.
- Statistics substituted for the person's own words ("1 in 4 children like Marcus...") crowding out
  their actual voice.
- Aggregated/composite "representative" stories presented as a single real individual without
  disclosure.
- Lack of a name, or use of only a diagnosis/condition/need as the identifying label ("a homeless
  man," "an at-risk youth").

## Consent and Interview Process (Standard Deliverable: Story Consent Protocol)

A defensible protocol has five parts — build this as a one-page document if the org doesn't have one:

1. **Informed, specific consent.** Consent must name the specific uses (which channels, how long,
   whether it can be reused in future campaigns) — a signature for "the newsletter" does not cover a
   paid social ad a year later. For minors, obtain guardian consent AND age-appropriate assent from
   the minor themselves.
2. **Right of review.** The subject sees the final draft/edit (and photo selection) before
   publication and can request changes or withdraw, not just approve a transcript.
3. **Power-aware interviewing.** The interviewer discloses their role, avoids leading questions that
   presuppose gratitude or trauma, and lets the subject set boundaries on topics. Never interview a
   current client alone in a context where declining feels risky to their services (have a
   non-service staff member present or conduct interviews after service completion when feasible).
4. **Compensation/reciprocity norm.** Decide and disclose the org's standard practice (token gift
   card, meal, transportation cost reimbursement) — treat storytelling time as a request for the
   person's labor, not a favor they owe the org for services rendered.
5. **Withdrawal path.** A named contact and process for someone to request a story be taken down
   later, and a tracking log of where each story/photo has been published so takedown is actually
   executable.

## Step-by-Step: Producing a Story

1. Identify the *purpose* first (gala remarks, grant narrative, web page, appeal insert) — length,
   tone, and which detail to foreground depend on the placement; don't write one generic story and
   force-fit it everywhere.
2. Select a subject who is willing, briefed on the ask, and — where possible — someone who has some
   time/distance from acute crisis so they aren't narrating trauma in real time under pressure.
3. Run the consent conversation before the interview, not after.
4. Interview using open, non-leading prompts: "What would you want someone reading this to
   understand?" "What's something people usually get wrong about your situation?" rather than
   "How did our program save you?"
5. Draft using the dignity-centered arc above. Read it back for the savior-framing checklist.
6. Route the draft to the subject for review and sign-off; log the approved scope of use.
7. Hand off the approved narrative to the relevant channel skill — annual report layout
   (`nonprofit-annual-report`), grant proposal narrative section (`nonprofit-grant-writing`), or
   appeal copy (`nonprofit-annual-appeals`) — rather than reformatting it yourself in this skill.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Composite/representative stories presented as literal fact** without disclosure — a legal and
  trust risk when donors later learn "Maria" is a blend of three clients.
- **Consent creep**: a story approved for one newsletter reused in a fundraising ad two years later
  without re-confirming consent or the person's continued willingness (circumstances, and their
  feelings about visibility, change).
- **Photo-text mismatch**: dignified text paired with an old stock photo or a candid shot chosen for
  emotional intensity rather than what the subject approved.
- **One story doing all the work**: relying on a single "hero client" repeatedly because they're
  comfortable being public, distorting the org's public narrative and over-exposing one person.
- **Skipping internal review**: program staff closest to the relationship should sanity-check drafts
  for accuracy and re-traumatization risk before communications finalizes copy.

## Practitioner vs. Advisor Framing

- **As the practitioner** (development/communications staff): use the consent protocol and dignity
  checklist directly as your production workflow before every published story; keep the withdrawal
  log current.
- **As an advisor/consultant**: introduce the savior-framing checklist as a governance tool — propose
  it become a board-adopted storytelling policy (pairs with `nonprofit-bylaws-policy` for
  formalizing it), and use an audit of the client's last 5-10 published stories against the checklist
  as a low-cost, high-credibility diagnostic deliverable early in an engagement.
