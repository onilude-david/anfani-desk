# Field guide: fellowships, scholarships, leadership programmes and awards

Researched October 2026. Deadlines, rules and policies change: check each programme's own page before acting.

How to fill any of them well, from about a hundred opened pages (list at the end). It assumes the standard
storytelling frameworks (STAR, ABT, public narrative, impact statements) and does not re-teach them.
Where a fact rests on a third-party listing rather than the programme's own page, the table says "3P".
Where a cycle is old, the year is given. Check the live call before any deadline is trusted.

---

## 1. How selectors decide

**The funnel is the same almost everywhere.** Five steps, in this order:

1. **Eligibility screen.** Staff, not judges. Mechanical and unforgiving. Missing documents end the file
   (Commonwealth: an application without every document is ineligible; Diana: only complete entries are
   reviewed; OYW: skip a section and the file is not considered).
2. **Reader scoring against the published criteria.** Usually two or more readers, scores averaged. Africa's
   Business Heroes uses two judges per file and ranks on the average. Tony Elumelu has an outside firm (EY)
   run three levels of review against fixed weights. MIT Solve uses several reviewers plus a staff member.
3. **A live test.** Interview, pitch, selection conference or group exercise. Its main job is verification:
   did this person write the file, and do the claims hold up under "how exactly did you do that?"
4. **Due diligence.** Police clearance and bank reference (Forbes Africa), PwC checks (ABH), embassy calls to
   referees (Chevening), safeguarding and media checks (Diana), Rhodes referees may be contacted.
5. **Portfolio balancing.** The last cut is a class, not a ranking. Forbes adjusts the final 30 for company type,
   gender and background. McCain aims for five per region. Global Shapers hubs cap any one gender at 60 percent
   and any one employer at three members. OYW gives priority to under-represented countries and first-timers.
   The UN Young Leaders call names rural youth, refugees and people with disabilities as groups it seeks.

**What this means for writing.**

- **The first reader is a generalist with a rubric.** Diana's judges ask nominators to explain every acronym and
  organisation. Echoing Green asks for plain language and no jargon. Write for a smart outsider.
- **Every criterion needs its own visible evidence.** Readers score line by line. TEF's published bands show the
  mechanics: a strong business that is missing a revenue model, a marketing model or visible leadership drops a
  whole band for each gap. One weak criterion costs more than one brilliant one earns.
- **Scale of competition is brutal and mostly invisible.** A CSIS panel of Mandela Washington fellows put 2024
  applications at about 53,000. One Nigerian fellow said roughly 10,000 Nigerians applied for about 55 places,
  with about 200 interviewed (anecdotal, one speaker). Assume a 1 to 2 percent rate for anything famous.
- **The interview checks the file, it does not replace it.** MWF alumni and Chevening interviewers say the same
  thing: questions come from what you wrote. Write nothing you cannot expand for five minutes.
- **Nomination gates are a separate game.** Ford Global Fellowship, WEF Young Global Leaders, Goalkeepers awards,
  the Diana Award, Gates Cambridge (department) and Watson (campus) only see nominated people. The work there is
  being known to nominating partners, not writing a better essay.

**The six things almost every rubric is measuring**, whatever the words:

| Family | Typical wording | Where it appears |
|---|---|---|
| Evidence of results | track record, measurable impact, quality of evidence | Acumen, Commonwealth Youth Awards, UN Young Leaders, Diana, ISOC |
| Personal agency | leadership, influence, youth-led, ownership | Chevening, Diana ("youth led"), Echoing Green, KHS |
| Proximity and purpose | relationship to the issue, purpose alignment, commitment | Echoing Green, Ford, OYW |
| A plan that follows | vision, career plan, leadership action plan, project plan | Chevening, McCain, ISOC, Acumen |
| Contribution to the cohort | networking, partnership, engagement with the network | Chevening, MWF, Eisenhower, Schwarzman |
| Character | integrity, ethical fibre, humility, the Rhodes virtues | Rhodes, KHS, Ashoka, YGL |

**What alumni and readers say separates winners.**

- **Show it, never define it.** Chevening's Reading Committee named defining leadership as the most common error.
  Holding a title was not accepted as proof of influence. Recent professional examples beat school ones.
- **Less polish, more person.** Schwarzman's admissions director warned against materials that feel overly
  rehearsed and against leadership essays about a task done well or an election won with no change after it.
  KHS readers want answers so personal that only you could have written them.
- **Name the limits.** Rhodes tells referees that committees distrust appraisals that imply no flaws. KHS asks
  recommenders for growth areas. A candidate who names a real gap reads as more credible.
- **One theme, not a list.** Diana's judges ask for the overarching theme of the work rather than an
  activity-by-activity list. Echoing Green's older guidance looked for "distance travelled" and the ability to
  attract people and money, not stage charisma.
- **Persistence pays.** The MWF fellows on the CSIS panel had reapplied, used a YALI Regional Leadership Centre
  first, and learned to tell the story better the second time.
- **Numbers that prove, not decorate.** Diana's judges say they love data and evidence; Ikenna Anyadike (MWF)
  advises carrying the statistics into the interview to defend the file.

## 2. Prompt bank

The recurring prompts, grouped. Each entry: real prompts (paraphrased), what the reader is testing, and an
answer structure. The structures sit on top of STAR, ABT and public narrative;
they do not replace them.

### 2.1 Leadership

**Seen as.** Chevening: lead or influence others, with results. Schwarzman: 750-word leadership essay. MWF:
an accomplishment from the last two years with measurable results; an unpopular decision. Echoing Green: an
example of building and managing an organisation toward strategic goals. KHS: a moment of acting with courage.
Diana: how the nominee led, owned and shaped the work. Gates Cambridge: capacity for leadership so far and later.

**What it tests.** That other people changed what they did because of you. Not your title.

**Structure: the influence arc.**
1. The position you held, and the limit of its authority (one sentence).
2. The resistance or gap: who did not agree, what was missing.
3. Your move: the specific thing you said or built that shifted them.
4. The result, counted, with a date.
5. What you would do differently (one sentence; Schwarzman and KHS reward this).
6. The bridge: how this habit carries into the programme or plan.

**Traps.** "We" without your part (Chevening); a crisis with no conflict; a list of roles; leadership defined.

### 2.2 Impact and results

**Seen as.** OYW: results to date, statistics, testimonials. Diana: social impact on others, evidenced with
facts. UN Young Leaders: tangible, measurable outcomes from your work. Commonwealth Youth Awards: level of
impact and quality of evidence. Echoing Green: how lives in the community will improve.

**What it tests.** Whether change happened, whether you caused it, and whether you know the difference.

**Structure: claim, proof, limit.**
1. One outcome with a baseline and a period (see section 7 of the storytelling file for outputs vs outcomes).
2. The proof type: register, survey, third-party report, testimonial. Name the source.
3. One human case that shows the number (with consent).
4. What you have not yet shown and how you will measure it.

**Traps.** Reach counts as impact; numbers with no source; claiming a whole community's change.

### 2.3 Failure, setback, falling short

**Seen as.** KHS: a time you fell short of expectations. MWF: an unpopular decision; overcoming adversity.
Chevening interview: a challenge or failure and the lesson. Diana: personal challenges overcome on the journey.
Echoing Green (older): resilience and distance travelled.

**What it tests.** Self-awareness, and whether the lesson changed later behaviour. GradPilot's reading of the KHS
prompt: falling short needs a real gap between what you intended and what happened, not a catastrophe.

**Structure: gap, cause, change, proof.**
1. What you intended and by when.
2. What happened instead (plain, no softening).
3. The cause you own (not the economy, not the team).
4. The specific rule or habit you changed.
5. A later moment where the new habit worked.

**Traps.** A disguised strength ("I work too hard"); blaming others; a lesson with no later evidence.

### 2.4 Vision and plan

**Seen as.** Chevening: short, mid and long-term plans with measurable goals. MWF: a 12-month initiative after
return; a US partnership with a first step in six months. McCain: a Leadership Action Plan. ISOC: a locally
grounded project plan built during training. KHS: short and long-term intentions (250 words). Commonwealth:
expected outcomes, timeframe, beneficiaries, measurement.

**What it tests.** Whether the plan follows from the past, whether it is specific enough to fail, and what
exactly the programme adds.

**Structure: three horizons and a hinge.**
1. Now: the one problem you are already working on (one line, so the plan has roots).
2. Six to twelve months: a dated first step with a named partner or resource.
3. Three to five years: an outcome with a number and a beneficiary group.
4. Ten years or more: the role or system change, stated modestly.
5. The hinge: the single thing the programme supplies that the plan cannot get elsewhere.

**Traps.** Titles instead of outcomes (GradPilot on KHS); a plan with no rationale (Chevening); a goal so large
nobody could check it.

### 2.5 Why this programme, why us

**Seen as.** Chevening: why these UK courses, with modules. Gates Cambridge interview: motivation and the
responsibilities of a full-cost award. OYW partner scholarships: why this organisation's cohort. MWF interview:
why this fellowship rather than another route. Obama Scholars: a breakthrough moment in your career.

**What it tests.** Research, fit, and whether you will give back to the cohort.

**Structure: gap, element, exchange.**
1. The gap you cannot close alone (a skill, a network, a proof point).
2. The named element that closes it: a module, a mentor type, a convening, the alumni in your sector.
3. What you give: a skill or network the cohort lacks (Chevening asks applicants to show, not promise, this).

**Traps.** Rankings and prestige; brochure language (Chevening readers spot it); no exchange offered.

### 2.6 Community, proximity and power

**Seen as.** Echoing Green: your relationship to the community; how you shift power and involve people in design
and decisions. Ford: proximate leaders addressing root causes of inequality. Diana: how the nominee works with
school, youth groups and businesses. MWF: volunteering and mentorship. OYW: your own story and why you care.

**What it tests.** Whether you work with people or on them.

**Structure: who, how close, who decides.**
1. The people, described specifically (place, age, situation), not "the youth".
2. Your proximity: lived, built, or earned over time. Say which.
3. One decision the community changed: a feature, a schedule, a location.
4. The structure that keeps them in the loop (a council, trainers drawn from alumni, feedback loop).

**Traps.** Rescue language; the community as backdrop; no example of a decision they made.

### 2.7 A global issue or a problem in your field

**Seen as.** Rhodes: from your place in the world, how you will use your talents on humanity's pressing
challenges. MWF: a challenge in your sector. Echoing Green: the root causes and why the problem still exists;
what kinds of power keep it in place. ISOC: link local problems to global Internet issues. Gates Cambridge
interview: an ethical question with no right answer.

**What it tests.** Thinking, not just doing. Whether you understand causes and can hold a position.

**Structure: local to systemic to lever.**
1. One local fact you have seen yourself.
2. The pattern it belongs to (named, with one external source).
3. The root cause or power that keeps it in place.
4. Your lever: the part you can move, and what you will not attempt.
5. One honest trade-off.

**Traps.** Statistics without a cause; solving the whole continent; no position taken.

### 2.8 Personal statement and "who are you"

**Seen as.** KHS: connect the dots, how influences shaped you (550 words); eight improbable facts (150 words).
Rhodes: 1,000 words on three guiding questions (your strongest Rhodes quality; communities at Oxford; your
energy on global challenges). Commonwealth: personal statement. Every interview: "Tell us about yourself."
Echoing Green and Schwarzman: a 60-second video.

**What it tests.** Who the person is when the CV is removed.

**Structure: two or three influences, each with a scene.**
1. An influence (a person, a place, a constraint) shown in one scene.
2. What it changed in how you act, proved by a later choice.
3. Repeat once or twice. Then one line on where these meet now.

For "improbable facts": range over rank. Mix the human (a habit, a skill outside work, a place) with one work
fact. No awards. For a 60-second video: one moment, one sentence of purpose, one ask. Echoing Green's video
prompt is simply why you do what you do.

**Traps.** A chronological CV in prose; a single trauma essay; three prompts answered with the same story.

### 2.9 Networking and partnership (a Chevening and MWF speciality)

**Seen as.** Chevening: how you build and use professional relationships, with outcomes. MWF: past and future
work with US organisations. Echoing Green: partners now and hoped for.

**Structure.** One relationship you built from nothing → what it produced → how you keep it alive → which
specific network in the programme you will use, and for what.

**Traps.** Joining an online group (Chevening called this weak); "I am a people person".

## 3. References and recommenders

### 3.1 What the programmes ask for

| Programme | Number and type | Special rules |
|---|---|---|
| Chevening | Two; professional or academic; not friends or family | Applicant collects letters; upload at least seven working days before interview; addressed to the embassy; may be verified; changing referees must be explained at interview |
| Rhodes (Global form, 2026) | Character and academic referees | Confidential; online form only; academic referees rank the applicant (top 1, 3, 5, 10, 25 percent) and state the comparison group size; must speak of limitations |
| Knight-Hennessy | Two | Rate on 11 traits by percentile; name the comparison group; five prompts; growth areas expected; 250 to 750 words typical; the recommender alone completes it |
| Schwarzman | Three | Writers who know you well; across the three, cover intellect and leadership |
| Commonwealth | Three on the form; at least two signed on letterhead | Must arrive by the main deadline |
| Humphrey | Two | One must be from the current employer |
| Atlas Corps (2019) | Two professional | Requested through the system at Part 2 |
| Diana Award | Nominator plus two supporters (2026); 2023 asked for a 200-word beneficiary testimonial | Nominator must know the nominee professionally; supporters use a template; no family or friends |
| Obama Leaders Africa | Two referees contacted by the Foundation | Letters not accepted |
| Ford Global, YGL, Goalkeepers | Nominator, not a referee | Self-nomination not accepted |

### 3.2 What a strong letter contains

Drawn from the Rhodes referee guidance, the KHS recommender page, and fellowship offices at Pomona, Stony Brook
and others. A letter that works has all eight:

1. **Vantage point.** How the writer knows the candidate, in what role, for how long, how recently.
2. **A central claim** in the first paragraph: the one quality that makes this person right for this award.
3. **A comparison group.** "Among the N founders I have funded" or "top 5 percent of the 120 in this cohort".
   KHS and Rhodes both ask for it in the form.
4. **Two or three anecdotes**, each tied to a named criterion. Specific beats glowing (Pomona: committees are
   sceptical of effusive, unqualified praise).
5. **Something the file does not already say.** Rhodes tells referees the committee already has the CV and
   statements; add what those cannot show.
6. **A growth area** and how the programme would help. Rhodes and KHS both ask for it.
7. **Fit with the programme's mission**, in the writer's words.
8. **Written by the referee.** KHS and Rhodes say the applicant must not draft it. A letter that reads like the
   applicant's essay is a red flag.

### 3.3 How to brief a referee

Six to eight weeks ahead, one email, one page attached:

- The programme, the deadline, the submission route and whether the letter is confidential.
- The criteria, copied from the programme's page.
- Which two or three criteria this referee is best placed to cover (assign different criteria to different
  referees so letters corroborate rather than repeat; Schwarzman says the set should cover both intellect and
  leadership).
- The one or two stories the candidate is using that the referee witnessed, with dates and numbers, so the
  referee can confirm detail without copying wording.
- A current CV and the draft essays, marked as drafts (Stony Brook advice).
- A reminder to include a comparison group and a growth area.
- Never a drafted letter. For Rhodes and KHS that breaks the rules; for the rest it weakens the letter.

### 3.4 Founder-specific reference problems

- **No employer above you.** Use the board chair, a lead funder, a long-standing client or a programme partner who
  saw the work. For Humphrey's "current employer" letter, a board chair on letterhead is the honest route.
- **Self-employment as work experience.** Chevening and DAAD count only listed employment types. A
  referee letter that states hours and role over a period does double duty.
- **Prestige versus proximity.** KHS says the strongest letters come from people who worked with you closely and
  recently, not from famous names. Rhodes says the same.

## 4. Interviews

### 4.1 Formats

| Programme | Format | Length | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chevening | Panel at the British embassy or high commission, in person or video | Not published on the pages read | Topics: essay examples, how the award serves impact goals, the three courses and offer status |
| Mandela Washington | In person at the US embassy or consulate, November to January | Third parties: about 20 to 30 min; one 2015 blog's commenters reported 10 to 20 min | Panels have mixed Nigerian and American staff; bring passport; arrive early |
| Gates Cambridge | Subject panel (arts, biological, physical, social sciences), in person or virtual | 25 to 30 min | Four parts: welcome; the scholarship; academic and career plans; your questions. Includes ethical questions with no right answer |
| Schwarzman | Panel of five leaders plus a group problem-solving exercise | About 25 min | Conversational; builds on the file; current affairs likely |
| Knight-Hennessy | Video statement by invitation, then the finalist weekend | | Playbook covers it |
| Acumen West Africa | Selection conference for finalists | Two days (2023, virtual) | Elevator pitches, group activities, panel interview |
| Africa's Business Heroes | Video call with two business leaders | About 90 min | Deep company assessment; top 20 then PwC checks |
| Atlas Corps | Selection board interview, then host organisation interviews | | Two separate interviews |
| Echoing Green | Finalist panel with expert panellists | | Early in the year after application |
| Watson | One hour with a foundation representative | 60 min | After campus nomination |
| Rhodes | Interviews plus a social engagement | | No AI tools during any interview or social event |
| MIT Solve | Live pitch and Q&A for finalists | | Technical vetting before it for some challenges |

### 4.2 Question bank

Gathered from programme pages, alumni posts and fellowship-office guides. Expect the first and last in almost
every interview.

**Opening**
1. Tell us about yourself. (Chevening alumni: say who you are and what that means for your country, not a list.)
2. Who are you, and what do you do? (MWF opener reported by fellows.)
3. Walk us through your plans for this year.

**Leadership and character**
4. Describe a time you influenced people without formal authority.
5. Tell us about your leadership experience.
6. Describe a decision you made that was unpopular. What happened?
7. Tell us about a failure and what changed after it.
8. How have you handled diversity or disagreement in a team?
9. Who is your role model, and why?
10. What is your weakness?

**The work and its impact**
11. Describe something new you built and how it helped people.
12. How do you know your work made a difference? What would you measure next?
13. How did you do that, exactly? (The follow-up on any claim.)
14. What volunteer work do you do in your community?

**Fit and motivation**
15. Why this programme and not another route?
16. Why this track or category?
17. How will you contribute to the cohort or network?
18. Name three things you expect to gain.
19. What will you do if you are not selected? (Chevening alumni named this the hardest.)
20. What does this award mean to you? Why should we invest in you?

**Plans**
21. What will you do in the first six months after the programme?
22. Where will you be in five and ten years?
23. How will your country benefit?
24. Why will your proposal actually make a difference?

**Thinking on your feet**
25. A current-affairs question you cannot predict (Schwarzman, reported by a third-party guide).
26. An ethical dilemma with no right answer (Gates Cambridge).
27. A hypothetical in your field: what would you do if funding halved?
28. Are you proud of your country? (Reported in MWF interviews, 2016–17.)

**Close**
29. Is there anything we have not asked that we should know?
30. Do you have questions for us? (Have one that shows expertise; MWF alumnus Adebayo Alonge advised a
    question that starts a discussion.)

### 4.3 How to prepare

1. **Audit every claim in the file.** For each number, date and outcome, write the two-minute "how". The
   interview is built from the application.
2. **Three stories, tagged.** Choose three stories and tag each to the criteria it proves (Central
   Arkansas advice). Most questions can be answered with one of them.
3. **A 30-second introduction** and a closing statement, learned as points, never memorised as text.
4. **Front-load answers.** Lead with the point, then the evidence. Panels interrupt.
5. **Mock interviews with interruptions.** Ask the mock panel to cut in. One Schwarzman scholar from NTU did
   repeated mocks with friends and mentors and read widely on global affairs beforehand.
6. **Know the programme's own commitments.** Return-home rules, service obligations, cohort duties. Committees
   want to see you have thought about them (Marshall University advice).
7. **Current affairs.** Hold a defensible view on two or three issues in your sector and country.
8. **Talk to alumni** from your country about how the local panel runs.
9. **AI is fine for practice, never in the room.** Rhodes allows AI to generate practice questions and bans any
   AI tool during interviews and social events.
10. **Logistics.** Passport for embassy interviews, arrive 30 minutes early, test video set-ups, professional
    dress (Central Arkansas notes the camera usually shows only the top half; dress fully anyway).

**Rules for this desk, by category.**

- **Forbids (Chevening, OYW, Schwarzman).** Record-only mode: the record only: facts, dates, numbers, documents, and for each question what it tests and the criteria in the programme's words. No drafted text, no outline, no suggested structure. The applicant writes every word; the desk may check facts and limits, never edit wording.
- **Rhodes.** No one may write or edit the statements, human or desk. AI may cut length and check grammar, but the
  tool and the prompts must be disclosed. The desk's role ends at the record and at general feedback.
- **Knight-Hennessy.** The desk must not hand the applicant an AI-made outline or narrative frame. It may supply the fact
  record, flag unclear passages without rewriting them, and check grammar.
- **Disclose (MWF, ISOC).** Desk help is limited to mechanics; the form's disclosure box is filled honestly.
- **Welcomes thoughtful use (Echoing Green).** The desk may help with structure and language; every story,
  reflection and claim comes from the applicant and reads in their voice.
- **Silent.** Treat as disclose. Substance from the applicant; the desk edits for clarity.
