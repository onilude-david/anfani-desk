# Question bank: how to fill any application form

Researched October 2026. Deadlines, rules and policies change: check each programme's own page before acting.

The core of the field guide. It teaches the desk to read any form that arrives (grant, fellowship, scholarship,
award, accelerator, job, speaking slot, visa or talent route, government or corporate programme, competition,
nomination) and to answer it from the record. Every source is listed in section 6.

How to use it. Section 1 says how the reader on the other side works. Section 2 names the question in front of you
and gives its structure. Section 3 is the checklist for the form itself. Section 4 is the AI and originality line.
Section 5 is the procedure, start to finish. Voice and facts still come from the applicant's profile file
`desk/profile.md`; this file decides shape, not content.

Conventions. Question wordings are close paraphrases of what each programme printed, tagged with a short source code
(legend in section 6). A tag starting `S-` means the wording was seen in search-result extracts of that page, not on
the page itself; check it live before relying on it. Quotation marks mean the exact words, always under 15 words.

## 1. How any reviewer reads an application

### What the evidence says

- **Volume forces speed.** YC partners read about 1,000 applications in roughly ten days, around 100 a day, and give
  the idea about 20 seconds on first look before moving to the founders [YC]. A CV gets about 7.4 seconds on the first
  screen; readers scan titles and headings in an F or E shape and drop cluttered, multi-column pages [HRD].
- **Triage comes before judgement.** Solve staff first screen every application for completeness, coherence and fit
  to the challenge; only then do expert reviewers score, and only the top 50 go on, then the top 15 [SOLVE]. The
  Commonwealth Scholarship Commission disqualifies incomplete applications every year and decides only on what is in
  the form [CSC]. NIH does not discuss every application; the weaker part gets no score at all [CS-NIH].
- **Two or three readers, then an average or a vote.** At an NIHR-style panel each application had two or three
  assigned readers before the meeting, then a timed slot of ten minutes (seven for some schemes): the lead reader
  summarised for about half of it, others questioned, and the whole panel voted [NIHR]. Africa's Business Heroes gave
  each first-round application two judges and ranked by the average [S-ABH]. NIH reviewers score 1 (exceptional) to 9
  (poor), and the panel's average times ten becomes the 10–90 impact score [CASRAI].
- **Readers disagree more than applicants think.** In a mock NIH study 43 expert reviewers rated the same 25 funded
  cancer proposals and showed no agreement on quality, in scores or in written critiques; who reviewed a proposal
  mattered more than the proposal [IHE][OBZ]. They also disagreed on how many weaknesses equal a given score. The
  practical answer is clarity: write so a reader outside your niche reaches the same conclusion as one inside it [OBZ].
- **Rubrics follow the form.** Good rubric design puts the scoring sections in the same order as the questions, keeps
  hard requirements as pass/fail, weights criteria by mission (one sample: need 25, academics 20, essay 25, community
  20, letters 10), defines each score band in words, and pilots with two readers; a gap above 10–15% sends the
  descriptors back for sharpening [KAL]. Reviewers are calibrated in a short session before live scoring [KAL]. Missing
  evidence is marked "not assessed", not guessed [SOP].
- **Readers are told to read twice and to use a fixed list of questions.** Inclusive-review guidance asks reviewers to
  read once for the overview and again for detail, with a standard question list and a standard rubric [PITT].
- **Claims get checked.** Forbes background-checks and fact-checks every nominee; one honoree was asked for three
  years of tax returns and references [F30][VP]. Echoing Green gives non-selected applicants numeric feedback on how
  their answers scored [EG]. Global Talent letters must come from experts who have known the work for at least 12
  months [GT].
- **Anonymous first rounds exist.** PyCon AU and DjangoCon strip names before the first scoring round and reveal them
  only when building the programme; reviewers flag proposals they recognise and skip them [PYCON][DJ].
- **Machines are joining the reading.** UK funders (UKRI, Wellcome and others) said in June 2026 they would let
  themselves use generative AI to process applications, with humans keeping the decision [RS]. Expect summaries and
  criterion matching to happen before a person reads.

### What this means for every answer

1. **The first sentence carries the score.** Put the answer to the question there, in plain words. YC asks for what
   you are building in the first sentence, without marketing language [YC].
2. **Match the criterion, by name, in the order asked.** The reader holds a rubric that mirrors the form. If the
   criterion is "measurable outcomes", the word outcome and a number must be findable in seconds.
3. **One example, fully told, beats three listed.** Leadership essays are not lists of accomplishments [OSU].
4. **Every claim must survive a check.** Dates, numbers, titles and names will be verified by someone, sometimes by
   asking for documents.
5. **Write for the outside reader.** Plain language, no acronyms without a gloss, the "so what" stated. Commonwealth
   panels mark down plans a non-specialist cannot follow [CSC].
6. **Use the space you are given, never more.** A 50–100-word answer in a 500-word field rarely persuades [CSC].
   Over the limit can disqualify outright [SS].
7. **Answer the question asked, not the one you prepared.** Readers score fit to the prompt. Reused text that misses
   the prompt reads as reused.
8. **Completeness is a gate.** A blank optional field, a missing reference or an unsigned declaration can end the
   application before any reader sees the essays.

## 2. The universal question taxonomy

Eighty-three question types in ten families. Each entry: wordings seen, what it really tests, the answer structure,
the common mistake, and how the answer changes at 50, 150, 300 and 500 words.

**Converting limits.** English prose runs about 7 characters per word including the space; Gates Cambridge pairs
1,400 characters with about 200 words [GC]. So 500 characters ≈ 70 words, 750 ≈ 105, 1,000 ≈ 140, 2,100 ≈ 300,
4,000 ≈ 570. Count the real thing in the real field before trusting the estimate (section 3).

**Length rules that apply to every entry.** At 50 words: one claim and one proof, no story. At 150: claim, one
concrete moment or number, consequence. At 300: the full structure with one example. At 500: the full structure, one
example told with a scene, a second piece of evidence, and the forward link. Never pad to the limit; aim for 90–100%
of it in scored essays and stop when the structure is complete.

### A. Who you are

**1. Tell us about yourself / personal statement**
- Seen: introduce yourself and say why you are a good candidate, with short- and long-term goals [VASG]; a narrative
  covering education, experience, interests, career plans and purpose of applying [S-FUL]; your background and what
  drives you to make an impact at home, including barriers overcome [CSC]; who you are, what your story is, what sets
  you apart [ABH]; personal statement of up to 1,000 words with the area of study and reasons for Oxford [RH].
- Tests: whether a person, not a CV, is behind the file; whether the life and the plan connect.
- Structure: one moment that explains the work → what you did about it → what it became → what you want next and why
  this programme is the next step.
- Mistake: a chronological CV in prose; opening with "I am"; adjectives instead of acts.
- 50: who you are in one line, the work in one line, the next step. 150: add the origin moment. 300: origin, one
  turning point, one result, the plan. 500: the 300 plus a scene and a second result.

**2. Short bio (tweet-length, 100 words, published profile)**
- Seen: a Twitter version of your bio [ABH]; biographical profile of 100 words or less, which will be published if
  selected [TRIN]; speaker profile kept apart from the anonymous abstract [PYCON]; a short bio as a standard field in
  nominations [VP].
- Tests: compression and judgement about what matters; it is often reused publicly.
- Structure: role and place → what you build → one result or distinction → (optional) what you are working on now.
- Mistake: stacking titles; including anything you would not want printed; mismatching the CV.
- 50: the approved short bio. 150 and above: rarely asked; use the approved long bio from `desk/profile.md`.

**3. Personal background / context**
- Seen: personal background, 500 words [CSCF]; how your background motivates you to make an impact at home [CSC];
  how you identify and where you are located, in an applicant-details section [EG26].
- Tests: proximity to the problem and what you overcame to get here.
- Structure: where you started → the constraint you lived with → the choice it led to → how it shapes the work now.
- Mistake: hardship told for sympathy with no link to the work; invented or exaggerated adversity.
- 50: place and constraint. 150: add the choice. 300: add one scene. 500: add how it shapes decisions today.

**4. Motivation: why this work, why this community**
- Seen: why you are passionate about this work and community [EG26]; why you are passionate about the problem and
  the population, and not because the problem is huge [EG20]; why are you dedicated to climate change, in 750
  characters [EG20]; why did you start the business [ABH]; video prompt "Why do you do what you do?" [EGV].
- Tests: staying power. Funders back people who will still be there in year five.
- Structure: the personal connection (a moment) → what it showed you → why you, not someone else → why still now.
- Mistake: Echoing Green names it: "the problem is so huge, I had to act" [EG20]. Also money or status as motive [ABH].
- 50: the connection in one sentence and the commitment in one. 150: add the moment. 300: moment, realisation, what
  you have done since. 500: add a cost you have paid to stay in it.

**5. Origin of the idea**
- Seen: when and how did you come up with the idea, who was involved, what motivates you, 500 characters [EG20];
  why did you start the business [ABH]; what drew you to this issue (common variant).
- Tests: that the idea came from contact with the problem, not from a trend.
- Structure: date and place → what you saw → the first small test → what it taught you.
- Mistake: a vague "I have always been passionate"; no date; no other people.
- 50: when, where, what you saw. 150: add the first test. 300: add who joined and what changed. 500: rarely needed.

**6. Values and character**
- Seen: values that define a hero (passion, boldness, resilience, being mission-driven, vision) and what they mean to
  you [ABH]; your leadership style and values against the Leadership Charter [OYW]; integrity, respect for others,
  energy to use one's talents [RH]; ethical fibre as an Ashoka criterion.
- Tests: whether your acts match their values. They are scoring alignment, not virtue.
- Structure: name one value they list → a time you acted on it at a cost → what you would do again.
- Mistake: listing their values back to them with no act attached.
- 50: one value, one act. 150: add the cost. 300: two values, one example each. 500: add how the value shapes your
  organisation's rules.

**7. Why you / why you deserve this**
- Seen: why do you believe you deserve to be named a business hero [ABH]; what makes your team a winning team [TS];
  why are you the leader to do this work (section framing) [EG20]; nominations explaining in detail why the person
  belongs on the list [VP].
- Tests: evidence of ability to deliver; self-awareness without false modesty. MWF tells applicants not to undersell.
- Structure: the claim (what you can do) → two proofs (done, with result) → the fit (why this, now).
- Mistake: superlatives; "I am the best"; or the reverse, hiding behind "we".
- 50: claim and best proof. 150: claim, two proofs, fit. 300: add the gap you will close. 500: add a story.

**8. Something impressive you have built or achieved**
- Seen: in one or two sentences, something impressive each founder has built or achieved [YC]; describe one
  accomplishment that shows leadership potential [EG20]; significant accomplishments that show achievement in your
  field [MRAC].
- Tests: the hardest thing you have done, measured. YC says do not name the startup itself; pick the hardest thing [YC].
- Structure: what it was → why it was hard → the result, in numbers or an outside marker.
- Mistake: generic praise ("excellent communicator"); picking the most recent instead of the hardest.
- 50: one sentence with a number. 150: add the difficulty. 300: add the decision that made it work. 500: rarely asked.

**9. Unconventional move / hacking a system**
- Seen: the time you most successfully hacked a non-computer system to your advantage [YC]; one example of your
  entrepreneurial spirit, 750 characters [EG20]; something new you launched and why you took the risk [EG20].
- Tests: resourcefulness and comfort with risk.
- Structure: the rule or constraint → the workaround → what it got you → whether it was fair.
- Mistake: a stunt with no outcome, or one that sounds like cheating.
- 50: the constraint and the move. 150: add the result. 300: add what you learned about systems. 500: rarely asked.

**10. Changed view of yourself / growth**
- Seen: an experience that changed how you think about yourself as a leader, what you learned and still work on
  [S-UT]; how the experience affected your leadership methods since [VASG]; since your last application, what has
  changed about you and your idea, 750 characters [EG20].
- Tests: reflection and the ability to change.
- Structure: before (what you believed) → the event → after (what you now do differently) → the open edge.
- Mistake: a lesson with no behaviour change; a flaw that is a disguised strength.
- 50: before and after. 150: add the event. 300: add the new practice and proof it stuck. 500: add the open edge.

### B. Fit with this programme

**11. Why this programme / fund / company**
- Seen: why you are applying for the prize, and how it fits your values [ABH]; how attending would grow your work
  [OYW]; reason for wanting to work at this company [WORK]; why this partner cohort [OYW]; how the proposal advances
  your goals [MRAC].
- Tests: research. Whether you know what they actually offer and will use it.
- Structure: one specific thing they offer → the gap it closes for you → what you will do with it in 12 months →
  the measurable change.
- Mistake: "prestigious", "honoured", praise of the brand; reasons that fit any programme.
- 50: the named offer and the change. 150: offer, gap, use. 300: add evidence you have done this before. 500: add the
  cohort or network you will draw on by name.

**12. Why now / timing**
- Seen: why is now the right time for your startup [TS]; inflection point, a system ready to shift [SKOLL]; why for
  the long term, why now (purpose alignment in Echoing Green).
- Tests: whether the moment is real or a habit of urgency.
- Structure: what changed recently (a fact with a date) → what it makes possible → the cost of waiting.
- Mistake: "the time is now" with no dated change.
- 50: the change and the window. 150: add the evidence. 300: add why you are positioned for it. 500: rarely asked.

**13. Course, institution or track choice**
- Seen: why this specific course and how it supports your plans, about 200 words [GC]; three researched courses with
  named modules, and the first choice explained [CHV]; detailed course and university plan, 300 words [CSCF]; choose
  only the tracks you really want; ticking every track hurts [PYCON]; pick one of three fellowship tracks [EG20].
- Tests: research depth. Rankings and "excellent university" are marked down [CSC].
- Structure: the skill gap → the module or track that closes it (by name) → where you will apply it → why not
  elsewhere.
- Mistake: copying the course page; generic reasons; listing options without a choice [CSC].
- 50: course and the one module that matters. 150: add the gap and the use. 300: add two modules and one person or
  lab. 500: add the comparison with alternatives.

**14. What you will contribute / give back**
- Seen: commitment to return to Sub-Saharan Africa and contribute (criterion) [MWF]; how you would contribute to UK
  digital technology (letters and statement) [GT]; how you will apply new skills after the award, 100 words [CSCF];
  what you hope to bring to and take from the fellowship [TRIN].
- Tests: reciprocity; whether the programme gets something back.
- Structure: what you bring now (skills, network, record) → how the cohort or country uses it → a dated commitment.
- Mistake: promises with no record behind them.
- 50: one asset and one commitment. 150: add a past instance. 300: add the mechanism. 500: add measures.

**15. What you hope to gain / skill to build**
- Seen: a skill you want to improve and how the fellowship helps [VASG]; what you lack (leadership frames in the
  answer library); how the Solve network would benefit the solution [SOLVE].
- Tests: honest self-assessment and a clear use for the support.
- Structure: the gap (specific) → why it matters now → how the programme fills it → how you will know.
- Mistake: a gap so big it reads as unready; or "nothing, I just want the money".
- 50: gap and use. 150: add stakes. 300: add evidence of past learning. 500: rarely asked.

**16. Partnership potential / how you will use the network**
- Seen: partnership potential, scored only at finalist stage; marked down if the only barrier is funding [SOLVE]; a
  demonstrated interest in building partnerships with US organisations [MWF]; Skoll additionality, how the award
  uniquely helps [SKOLL].
- Tests: that you need what they give beyond money.
- Structure: the named resource → the specific use → the result for the work.
- Mistake: saying money is the only thing you need [SOLVE].
- 50: one resource, one use. 150: two. 300: add a named partner type and timeline. 500: rarely asked.

**17. Previous application: what has changed**
- Seen: since your last application, what has changed about you and the idea [EG20]; have you applied before (yes/no
  administrative field, common variant); previous funding from this funder [MER].
- Tests: progress between attempts.
- Structure: the feedback or gap last time → what you did → the evidence of change.
- Mistake: resubmitting the same text; hiding a past rejection when the funder's records show it.
- 50: one change with a number. 150: add what prompted it. 300: rarely asked.

### C. Leadership and behaviour

**18. Leadership example**
- Seen: an experience where you used leadership to achieve something significant in your community [S-MV]; one or
  two times you stepped up to address a challenge [S-AHA]; examples of leadership capacity such as initiating change
  or bringing people together, about 300 words [GC]; a time you led a group through a challenge or a new creation,
  200 words [TRIN]; leadership essay, 750 words [SS].
- Tests: action, influence, result. Chevening wants clear results [CHV].
- Structure: STAR. Situation (one line) → Task (what you owned) → Action (most of the words, "I") → Result (number
  or outside marker) → Lesson (one line).
- Mistake: describing a role instead of an act; "we" throughout; no result; several examples skimmed.
- 50: action and result. 150: CAR. 300: STAR with one quote or detail. 500: STAR with a scene and the lesson applied
  later.

**19. Leading change / influencing others**
- Seen: taking the lead and influencing others with clear results (assessment area) [CHV]; strategic thinking,
  influencing others, leading change as angles [S-AHA]; how you engage others in support of your vision [EG26].
- Tests: persuasion without authority.
- Structure: the resistance → who you needed → how you moved them → what changed.
- Mistake: change by title or order; no resistance in the story.
- 50: who you moved and the result. 150: add the resistance. 300: full arc. 500: add a setback mid-way.

**20. Teamwork**
- Seen: a project completed as a team member, challenges faced and overcome [VASG]; ability to work cooperatively in
  groups (criterion) [MWF]; how you and your co-founder resolve conflict [EG20].
- Tests: whether you can be led as well as lead.
- Structure: the team and your role → the friction → what you did → the result and what you contributed.
- Mistake: being the hero of a team story.
- 50: role and result. 150: add friction. 300: full. 500: rarely asked.

**21. Building and managing an organisation**
- Seen: an example of your ability to build and manage an organisation toward strategic goals [EG26]; ability to
  deliver: leadership, governance, financial health [SKOLL]; full-time staff and operating history fields [ABH].
- Tests: operating skill, not vision.
- Structure: the goal → the system you built (people, money, process) → what it delivered → what you would change.
- Mistake: vision talk; no numbers on staff, budget or delivery.
- 50: what you built and its scale. 150: add one system. 300: add a hard decision. 500: add governance and finance.

**22. Attracting money, people and resources**
- Seen: skills or experiences that show you can attract money, people and other resources [EG20]; examples of
  coalition and resource mobilisation (Echoing Green criteria); share any
  revenue and funding you have [TS].
- Tests: fundraising and recruiting ability. Any past example counts [EG20].
- Structure: what you needed → who you asked and how → what you raised (cash, people, in-kind) → what it enabled.
- Mistake: no figure; confusing applying with raising.
- 50: the amount or the people, and the source. 150: add how. 300: two examples. 500: rarely asked.

**23. Networking and relationships**
- Seen: how you built strong working relationships and how collaboration led to measurable outcomes [CHV]; strong
  social and communication skills (criterion) [MWF]; how you engage others [EG26].
- Tests: whether your network produces results.
- Structure: the relationship (who, how it started) → what you did together → the measurable outcome → how you keep
  it.
- Mistake: name-dropping; counting contacts.
- 50: one relationship, one outcome. 150: add how it began. 300: two relationships. 500: add how you use the network
  for others.

**24. Helping others grow / mentoring**
- Seen: a time you helped someone learn, grow or gain confidence, and what you learned [S-UT]; trainees who became
  trainers (answer library frame); people you have developed (common variant in leadership forms).
- Tests: multiplying leadership.
- Structure: who they were → where they started → what you did → where they are now.
- Mistake: taking credit for their achievement.
- 50: the person and the change. 150: add what you did. 300: add a second person or the system. 500: rarely asked.

**25. Adversity / obstacle**
- Seen: one or two examples of overcoming adversity [EG20]; a time you overcame an obstacle, steps taken, what you
  learned [VASG]; barriers you overcame, personal or community [CSC]; resilience as a value [ABH].
- Tests: persistence and judgement under pressure.
- Structure: the obstacle (specific) → the stakes → the steps (decisions) → the outcome → the lesson.
- Mistake: trauma detail with no agency; an obstacle you did not actually overcome told as if you did.
- 50: obstacle, action, outcome. 150: add stakes. 300: full STAR. 500: add what you do differently now.

**26. Failure or mistake**
- Seen: a time that you failed, inside the adversity question [EG20]; disclose known flaws, because unmentioned
  problems suggest you have not seen them [YC]; challenges faced in a team project [VASG].
- Tests: ownership and learning. Reviewers trust applicants who name a real failure.
- Structure: what you decided → what went wrong (own it) → the cost → what you changed → evidence the change works.
- Mistake: a fake failure ("I worked too hard"); blaming others; no change.
- 50: the failure and the change. 150: add the cost. 300: full. 500: add the proof that the fix held.

**27. Strongest skill in action**
- Seen: a situation where you applied one of your strongest skills, and the outcome [VASG]; technical skills that
  demonstrate potential to lead [EG26 resume]; experience with specific software [WORK].
- Tests: self-knowledge, evidenced.
- Structure: the skill named → the situation → what you did with it → the result.
- Mistake: listing many skills; no outcome.
- 50: skill and result. 150: add the situation. 300: full. 500: rarely asked.

**28. Diversity, inclusion and working across difference**
- Seen: a time you worked in a diverse team and what you learned, 300 words [VASG]; a future where people of all
  identities have equal opportunity, and how your vision helped bring it [EG26]; respect for others (criterion) [MWF];
  selection that includes all kinds of talent [EG].
- Tests: practice, not statement. How you include people in decisions.
- Structure: the difference present → the friction or blind spot → what you changed in how you work → the result.
- Mistake: a values statement with no example; speaking for groups you do not belong to.
- 50: one practice and its effect. 150: add the situation. 300: full. 500: add organisational policy and data.

**29. Ethics and integrity**
- Seen: ethical fibre (Ashoka criterion); self-certification of no conduct
  violation [VASG]; consent and safeguarding for beneficiary stories; ability
  to defend every part if shortlisted [APHRC].
- Tests: judgement when the right thing costs something.
- Structure: the dilemma → the options → what you chose and why → what it cost → the rule you now follow.
- Mistake: an easy choice dressed as a dilemma.
- 50: dilemma and choice. 150: add the cost. 300: full. 500: add the policy that came from it.

### D. The problem, the solution, the model

**30. Problem statement**
- Seen: describe the problem, its root causes and why it still exists, 1,000 characters [EG26]; what problem is your
  company solving [TS]; outline the problem, how customers deal with it now and why current fixes fail [ABH]; the
  development issue your work addresses, 200 words [CSCF].
- Tests: evidence that the problem is real, local and understood. Echoing Green marks down a need stated without
  figures or sources, and a market gap passed off as a social problem [EG20].
- Structure: ABT. The situation and the people in it → but (the gap, with one sourced figure) → therefore (why it
  persists and why it matters now).
- Mistake: a pile of national statistics with no person in it; no cause; a problem defined as "lack of our product".
- 50: one sentence of gap, one of cause, one figure. 150: add who is affected and why it persists. 300: add the root
  cause and one local scene. 500: add why others have not solved it.

**31. Root causes, systems and power**
- Seen: what kinds of power shape the system and how your work tackles root causes [EG26]; system-level impact above
  the community level [SKOLL]; the causes of the problem and why it still exists [EG20].
- Tests: whether you treat causes or symptoms.
- Structure: the visible symptom → the cause beneath it → who holds the levers → which lever you pull and how.
- Mistake: jargon ("systemic", "ecosystem") with no named actor or rule.
- 50: cause and lever. 150: add who holds power. 300: add a sign the lever moves. 500: add the second-order effect.

**32. Solution and activities**
- Seen: what is your company going to make to solve the problem [TS]; the approach and the specific activities
  involved [EG26]; what you or staff will do daily, weekly, monthly, and how a person experiences it [EG20]; what
  would you like to do, with who benefits and for how long [S-NLCF]; major activities between start and end dates
  [MRAC].
- Tests: that the reader can picture the work. Echoing Green says the reader should know exactly what you do after
  reading [EG20].
- Structure: one-line description → who it is for → what happens, step by step, from their side → the evidence it
  works.
- Mistake: restating the idea instead of the operation; features without the user.
- 50: what, for whom, the key step. 150: add the user's journey. 300: add the evidence. 500: add delivery capacity.

**33. Community served / target audience**
- Seen: tell us about the community and the people you serve [EG26]; who is your target audience [TS]; the
  population your organisation will serve and their lives [EG20]; target audience and assumed knowledge for a talk
  [PYCON].
- Tests: closeness. Do you know them as people, not segments?
- Structure: who (specific) → where → what their day or constraint looks like → how you know (time with them, data).
- Mistake: "youth" or "everyone"; describing people only by deficits.
- 50: who, where, the constraint. 150: add how you know. 300: add one person's situation (with consent). 500: add
  sub-groups and how needs differ.

**34. What is new about the approach**
- Seen: how your work introduces a new approach or improves on existing ones [EG26]; new technology, new application,
  new business model or new process (criterion) [SOLVE]; new idea that changes the pattern in a field (Ashoka).
- Tests: difference relative to named alternatives. Solve marks down a copy with no change for context [SOLVE].
- Structure: what exists → what it misses → what you do differently → why that difference matters for results.
- Mistake: novelty claimed without naming what it is new compared to.
- 50: the difference in one line. 150: add the alternative. 300: add evidence the difference works. 500: add why it
  is hard to copy.

**35. Landscape: competitors, peers, influences**
- Seen: who are your competitors, current or future [TS]; name specific organisations in your field and how you
  differ [EG20]; what existing work has influenced you and what you do differently [EG26]; similar organisations and
  how you might work with them (collaborative analysis) [EG].
- Tests: homework and humility.
- Structure: two or three named players → what each does well → the gap → your position, and where you would partner.
- Mistake: "we have no competitors"; disparaging others [SNYK].
- 50: names and your difference. 150: add the gap. 300: add a partnership angle. 500: add a small comparison table if
  the form allows.

**36. Competitive advantage**
- Seen: what is your competitive advantage [TS]; what makes your team a winning team [TS]; what is distinctive about
  your approach and how you will handle obstacles [YC].
- Tests: why you win, specifically.
- Structure: the advantage (access, insight, team, cost, data) → proof → why it lasts.
- Mistake: "passion" or "quality" as the advantage.
- 50: the advantage and proof. 150: add durability. 300: two advantages. 500: rarely asked.

**37. Business model / revenue**
- Seen: how do you or will you make money, and how much could you make [TS]; financial viability and growth (Solve
  scalability) [SOLVE]; revenue as an eligibility gate [ABH].
- Tests: that someone pays, and that the numbers add up.
- Structure: who pays → for what → price and margin → current revenue → path to the target.
- Mistake: "ads and partnerships"; an invented projection presented as fact.
- 50: payer, price, current revenue. 150: add unit economics. 300: add the path. 500: rarely asked.

**38. Market size and opportunity**
- Seen: detailed market sizing (YC guidance relayed in search extracts) [S-YC]; how much could you make [TS]; size and
  growth of ventures as a judging factor [VP].
- Tests: realism. Bottom-up beats top-down.
- Structure: the reachable buyers you can count → price → the first market → the expansion.
- Mistake: "1% of a billion-person market".
- 50: the bottom-up figure. 150: add the method. 300: add expansion logic. 500: rarely asked.

**39. Customer acquisition / reach strategy**
- Seen: what is your customer acquisition strategy [TS]; how you engage the community and get people involved
  [S-NLCF]; reach and dissemination plan (common in grants).
- Tests: a channel that works now, with cost.
- Structure: the channel that already works (with numbers) → why it works → the next channel → the cost per person.
- Mistake: a list of every channel.
- 50: the working channel and its result. 150: add cost. 300: add the next channel. 500: rarely asked.

**40. Traction and results to date**
- Seen: share any traction you have [TS]; results to date [OYW]; evidence customers are buying [ABH]; three or more
  years of revenue as a gate [ABH].
- Tests: proof that the thing works outside your head.
- Structure: the strongest single number with its period → the trend → one user outcome → what it tells you.
- Mistake: vanity counts (followers, downloads) without use or retention.
- 50: one number with a date and one outcome. 150: add the trend. 300: add the user outcome and its source. 500: add
  what you learned and changed.

**41. Stage of development**
- Seen: choose the stage that matches the solution: concept, prototype, pilot, growth, scale [SOLVE]; the business
  must already run; ideas do not qualify [ANZ]; founding year and incorporation [TS]; registration date versus
  operating start [ABH].
- Tests: eligibility and honesty. Stage often gates the whole application.
- Structure: pick the stage the evidence supports; add one sentence of proof.
- Mistake: overstating stage; mixing up registration date and first operation [ABH].
- 50 and up: the stage, the proof, the next stage milestone.

**42. Technology and technical depth**
- Seen: technical feasibility: evidence the novel technology has been built and works [SOLVE]; top technical
  challenges the startup faces [GGA]; technical or business background for Global Talent [GT]; open-source work
  acknowledged by peers as evidence [GT].
- Tests: that it exists and that you understand it.
- Structure: what the system does → how it is built (stack, architecture in plain words) → what is hard and how you
  solved it → proof (repo, demo, users).
- Mistake: buzzwords; claiming AI where a rule does the job; unverifiable claims.
- 50: what it does and the hard part. 150: add how. 300: add proof and the next technical risk. 500: add architecture.

**43. Community involvement, co-design and power**
- Seen: how you shift power to the community and involve it in design and decisions [EG26]; how does the project
  involve your community, 50–150 words [S-NLCF]; designed with and for underserved communities (criterion) [SOLVE];
  how you know the community wants it: past delivery, local data, engagement [NLCF].
- Tests: participation, with evidence.
- Structure: how they were consulted (how many, how, what they said) → where they sit in decisions → what changed
  because of them.
- Mistake: "we consulted the community" with no who, how many or what. Connected Voice advises naming numbers,
  methods and real quotes [NLCF].
- 50: method, number, one change. 150: add their role in decisions. 300: add a quote and a change you made. 500: add
  governance.

### E. Impact and evidence

**44. Impact to date with evidence**
- Seen: results to date backed by figures and evidence [OYW]; impact record and commitment to learning [SKOLL];
  significant impact in an industry (nomination criterion) [F30]; evidence such as press, reports and testimonials
  [DIANA].
- Tests: outcomes, not outputs, and how you know.
- Structure: before → after → for whom and how many → over what period → how measured → one named example.
- Mistake: outputs as outcomes (hours delivered, people reached); attribution creep.
- 50: one outcome with baseline and period. 150: add method and one example. 300: add a second outcome and the limit
  of the claim. 500: add a testimony and what did not work.

**45. How lives will improve / outcomes**
- Seen: how will the lives of the people improve because of this work [EG26]; how lives will be better, with data,
  examples or hypotheses [EG20]; what will change in development terms after the award, 250 words [CSCF].
- Tests: a credible chain from activity to change.
- Structure: the outcome in a person's terms → the indicator → the target and date → why you believe it.
- Mistake: "empower", "transform" without a measurable change. Echoing Green's own example contrasts 500 tutoring
  hours (output) with a rise in college attendance (outcome) [EG20].
- 50: one outcome and indicator. 150: add the target. 300: add the causal chain. 500: add short- and long-term layers.

**46. Measurement, monitoring and evaluation**
- Seen: how will you track and assess status and success, 500 characters [EG20]; how you will measure the change, 100
  words [CSCF]; how you would know activities lead to the outputs and outcomes in the logframe (ISOC guidance, seen via search);
  set up monitoring so you can prove impact [NLCF].
- Tests: that you will know if it is not working.
- Structure: two or three indicators → baseline → data source and frequency → who reviews it → what you do with it.
- Mistake: measuring only activity; no baseline. Early-stage applicants may say what they plan to measure; Echoing
  Green accepts that [EG20].
- 50: the indicators and source. 150: add baseline and frequency. 300: add use of the data. 500: add independent
  checks.

**47. Theory of change / logic model**
- Seen: a logical theory of change backed by evidence (scoring note) [SOLVE]; causal pathways from early changes to
  outcomes, with assumptions and risks (funder template, via search); theory of change as assumptions, strategies,
  milestones and impact [INNO].
- Tests: coherence. Why these activities should cause that change.
- Structure: if (activities) → then (outputs) → so that (outcomes) → leading to (impact), with the assumptions named.
- Mistake: a diagram with arrows and no assumptions; impact claims beyond what the activity can reach.
- 50: the if-then chain in one sentence. 150: add the key assumption. 300: full chain with evidence per link. 500: add
  risks to each assumption.

**48. Development impact / benefit to your country**
- Seen: development impact in four fields: issue, applying skills, expected change, measurement [CSCF]; commitment to
  return and contribute [MWF]; how the UK digital sector benefits [GT]; what you would bring home (answer library).
- Tests: public benefit, not personal career gain. CSC marks down answers about personal success [CSC].
- Structure: the national issue → your specific contribution → the channel (job, organisation, policy) → the change
  and how measured.
- Mistake: claiming the subject is developmental by nature [CSC].
- 50: issue and contribution. 150: add channel. 300: add measure. 500: use the full space; short answers lose [CSC].

**49. Scale and growth plan**
- Seen: how you expect the organisation to grow over two years [EG20]; can the solution grow to affect more people
  (criterion) [SOLVE]; potential for system-level change and long-term growth [SKOLL].
- Tests: a realistic route, not ambition.
- Structure: current scale → the growth model (more sites, partners, licensing, product) → the constraint → the
  milestone and the money needed.
- Mistake: hockey-stick numbers with no mechanism.
- 50: model and target. 150: add the constraint. 300: add milestones. 500: add what will not scale and why.

**50. Sustainability beyond the grant**
- Seen: viable path to financial sustainability (criterion) [SOLVE]; sustainability as a step in finishing the
  application (ISOC guidance, via search); what continues when the grant ends (answer library frame).
- Tests: whether the money buys something lasting.
- Structure: what the grant builds → what keeps running and who pays → evidence of that payer.
- Mistake: "we will apply for more grants" as the plan.
- 50: the lasting asset and the payer. 150: add evidence. 300: add the transition timeline. 500: rarely asked.

### F. Plans, money and people

**51. Project description / what you would like to do**
- Seen: what would you like to do, 50–300 words (lottery guidance seen via search) [S-NLCF]; describe the proposal and
  its goals [MRAC]; name and title of the proposal, 50 characters [MRAC]; short description of 200 characters that
  may be reused in announcements [MRAC].
- Tests: clarity and fit with the funder's priorities.
- Structure: what → for whom → where and when → the difference it makes → why you.
- Mistake: describing the organisation instead of the project.
- 50: the one-paragraph summary. 150: add the difference. 300: add activities and evidence of need. 500: add
  partners and timeline.

**52. Funding priorities alignment**
- Seen: how your project meets our funding priorities; address one priority only and say which [NLCF]; alignment
  with challenge goals [SOLVE]; fit with the grant call and scope (reviewer check) [PITT].
- Tests: fit. Reviewers check scope first.
- Structure: name the priority in their words → the change your project makes on it → the evidence.
- Mistake: claiming every priority.
- 50: priority and change. 150: add evidence. 300: add how you will show it at the end. 500: rarely asked.

**53. Work plan and timeline**
- Seen: major activities between start and end dates [MRAC]; realistic, practical plan that fits the context
  (feasibility) [SOLVE]; how long you expect to run it [S-NLCF].
- Tests: feasibility.
- Structure: phases with months → key activity per phase → who does it → the milestone that closes each phase.
- Mistake: everything in month one; no slack; activities with no owner.
- 50: phases and end date. 150: add owners. 300: add milestones. 500: add dependencies and contingency.

**54. Milestones / next 12 months**
- Seen: your next major company milestones [TS]; objectives during the award, 250 words [CSCF]; 12-month goals
  (answer library frame).
- Tests: focus and measurability.
- Structure: three milestones, each with a date and a number, each tied to a goal.
- Mistake: tasks instead of results ("launch website").
- 50: three milestones. 150: add why these. 300: add how you get there. 500: rarely asked.

**55. Amount requested / total budget**
- Seen: how much money you need in two years to reach the growth described, in USD, digits and a period only
  [EG20]; amount requested within a band (common); budget ceiling and duration [S-NLCF].
- Tests: realism and fit with the funder's size.
- Structure: the number in the exact format asked → the period → (where text allowed) the two biggest lines.
- Mistake: format errors. Echoing Green's field rejected "$2,500" and "2,500 USD"; only 2500 or 2500.00 passed [EG20].
- 50 and up: rarely a text field; see 56.

**56. Budget justification / use of funds**
- Seen: budget narrative item by item with calculations [UVU]; personnel role, salary and time share; travel broken
  out [UVU]; test every line against the activity it supports [APHRC].
- Tests: that every cost serves the plan and is priced honestly.
- Structure: use the budget's own headings → per line: what, quantity × unit cost, why needed, which activity.
- Mistake: lump sums; padding (reviewers spot it and decline [UVU]); costs that appear in the budget but not in the
  narrative, or the reverse [UVU].
- 50: headline split by category in percentages. 150: the top three lines justified. 300: every category. 500: every
  line with the calculation.

**57. Revenue and funding to date / previous funding**
- Seen: share any revenue and funding you have [TS]; did your organisation receive our funding last year [MER];
  financial overview of the organisation [EG]; funding raised as a judging factor [F30].
- Tests: track record and dependency.
- Structure: total raised → by source and year → what it delivered → current runway.
- Mistake: hiding a past grant from the same funder; mixing pledges with received money.
- 50: totals by source. 150: add what it delivered. 300: rarely asked.

**58. Other funders, match funding and in-kind**
- Seen: match funds and in-kind support shown in the budget narrative [UVU via search]; cost-share only when required
  [UVU]; other sources of support (common).
- Tests: leverage and honesty.
- Structure: each other source → amount → status (confirmed, pending) → what it covers.
- Mistake: listing pending money as confirmed; volunteering cost-share the funder did not ask for [UVU].
- 50 and up: a short table if allowed.

**59. Risks and mitigation**
- Seen: what could make the project fail and the plan if it happens (training slide, via search); feasibility: a plan
  unlikely to succeed even with funding scores low [SOLVE]; assumptions and risks in a theory of change (template,
  via search).
- Tests: preparedness. Leaving out obvious risks reads as unaware or hiding [APHRC via search].
- Structure: three to five real risks → likelihood and impact → mitigation → fallback, each tied to a person or line.
- Mistake: "no major risks"; twenty generic risks.
- 50: top risk and mitigation. 150: three risks. 300: table of five. 500: add the risk you cannot mitigate and why
  you proceed.

**60. Partnerships**
- Seen: which organisations do you or will you partner with to deepen impact [EG26]; key partnerships as part of
  ability to deliver [SKOLL]; others involved in the proposal and how they help [MRAC].
- Tests: real partners with real roles.
- Structure: partner → role → status (signed, in talks) → what they add.
- Mistake: logos without agreements; naming a partner without permission.
- 50: partners and roles. 150: add status. 300: add what each brings. 500: add governance of the partnership.

**61. Team, roles and co-founders**
- Seen: how you started the organisation together, how long you have known each other, how you resolve conflict, 500
  characters [EG20]; your individual roles and working relationship [EG20]; working on the startup full time [TS];
  what makes the team a winning team [TS].
- Tests: that the team can deliver and will last.
- Structure: who does what (precise) → relevant record of each → how decisions and conflict are handled → time
  commitment.
- Mistake: vague co-leadership; hiding a part-time founder.
- 50: names, roles, time. 150: add record. 300: add conflict handling. 500: add gaps you will hire for.

### G. The future

**62. Career plan in horizons**
- Seen: short-, mid- and long-term plans with clear, measurable goals [CHV]; objectives during the award, the five
  years after, and long term, 250 words each [CSCF]; short-term (1–5 years) and long-term (over 5) goals [VASG]; how
  the role fits your long-term career [WORK].
- Tests: realism and a link to the programme.
- Structure: now → 1–2 years (named role or result) → 5 years → 10 years, each with a marker, each traceable to the
  programme.
- Mistake: a grand end state with no first step; vagueness.
- 50: the three horizons in one line each. 150: add markers. 300: add the link to the programme. 500: add the
  fallback path.

**63. Long-term vision**
- Seen: your future vision [OYW]; why you will keep leading in your field over the next ten years and more (nomination
  form, via search) [VP]; imagine a future of equal opportunity and how your vision helped [EG26].
- Tests: ambition with a mechanism.
- Structure: the world in 10 years → your part in it → the first proof that it is possible.
- Mistake: utopia with no role for you.
- 50: the picture and your part. 150: add the proof. 300: add the path. 500: rarely asked.

**64. Study or research objectives**
- Seen: study objective essay tied to the field, up to 600 words (Fulbright, via search) [S-FUL]; academic statement
  of study, 350 words [RH]; non-technical outline of the study, 125 words [CSCF]; research proposal for PhD
  applicants [GC].
- Tests: a clear question and a feasible method.
- Structure: the question → why it matters → the method → what you will produce → how it feeds your plan.
- Mistake: topic, not question; methods too technical for the panel [CSC].
- 50: question and outcome. 150: add method. 300: add fit with the place. 500: add timeline and risks.

### H. Declarations and administrative questions

**65. Eligibility and self-certification**
- Seen: self-certification of good standing and no conduct violation [VASG]; age, citizenship and residency checks
  with documents at interview [MWF]; nominee age on a set date [F30]; founder, African citizen, business running
  [ANZ]; four pre-application checks including three years of revenue [ABH].
- Tests: the gate. Fail it and nothing else is read.
- Structure: answer exactly; attach proof where asked.
- Mistake: "worth a try" on a hard gate; a date that does not match documents.
- No essay length. Record the gate and the proof in the sheet header.

**66. Conflict of interest**
- Seen: does anyone in your family hold a position at the funder; relationships with evaluators; evaluator interest in
  your organisation; funding from the funder last year [MER]; actual, potential or perceived conflicts, with names
  and relationships [MN].
- Tests: honesty. Non-disclosure can disqualify [MER]; disclosure alone does not [MN].
- Structure: yes or no per item; for each yes, who, the relationship, and the mitigation.
- Mistake: answering no to avoid a conversation.
- Text length: 50 words per conflict is enough.

**67. Awards, recognition and press**
- Seen: awards, competitions won, and whether you have been published [ABH]; press mentions and social handles
  (nomination form, via search) [VP]; a prize from a body of standing as Global Talent evidence [GT]; prizes and
  published material as O-1A criteria [O1].
- Tests: outside validation.
- Structure: name, awarding body, year, selection rate if known, one line on what it recognised.
- Mistake: inflating a shortlist into a win; listing paid or pay-to-enter "awards".
- List format; no essay.

**68. Safeguarding, ethics approval and data protection**
- Seen: protections for human subjects as a scoring factor [CS-NIH]; do not put others' personal data into AI tools
  without consent [UKRI]; consent before using a beneficiary's story.
- Tests: whether you protect the people you work with.
- Structure: the policy that exists (name, date) → who is responsible → how it is applied → what is planned if missing.
- Mistake: claiming a policy that does not exist. Say what will exist and by when.
- 50: the policy and owner. 150: add practice. 300: rarely asked.

**69. AI use and own-work certification**
- Seen: certify all written work is your own and no AI generator was used [SS]; disclose any technology used,
  including AI, where the form asks [MWF]; disclose substantive AI use [UKRI]; signed certification that the essay is
  your own work [RH].
- Tests: honesty, and compliance with the policy recorded in the sheet header.
- Structure: the exact disclosure the programme asks for, true to what happened.
- Mistake: certifying "no AI" when the desk drafted; see section 4.
- One or two sentences.

**70. Demographics and equal-opportunity monitoring**
- Seen: questions on how you identify, in applicant details [EG26]; voluntary disability self-identification with a
  "do not wish to answer" option, kept from hiring decision-makers [S-CC305]; anonymised CVs
  without name, age or gender [SP].
- Tests: usually nothing; they monitor the pool. Some programmes do use identity for targeted tracks.
- Structure: the applicant decides each answer themselves. Use "prefer not to say" where offered if they wish.
- Mistake: the desk guessing an identity field. Race, religion, health and sexual orientation are special-category
  data under UK GDPR [ICO-S]; never fill these from inference.
- No essay.

**71. Consent, data and publicity checkboxes**
- Seen: consent to share nominator name and email with the nominee [DIANA]; the short description may be edited for
  announcements [MRAC]; the bio will be published if selected [TRIN]; terms, privacy notices and marketing opt-ins
  (common).
- Tests: nothing to score; everything to get right.
- Structure: read each box. Valid consent is opt-in, specific, unbundled and withdrawable; a pre-ticked box is not
  consent [ICO-C]. Accept what the application requires; leave marketing opt-ins to the applicant.
- Mistake: ticking all; missing that a field will be published.
- No essay.

### I. Supporting materials

**72. Title and one-sentence summary**
- Seen: proposal name, 50 characters, and short description, 200 characters [MRAC]; what is your idea worth spreading,
  150 characters (TED, via search) [S-TED]; talk title and abstract [PYCON]; elevator pitch of two or three sentences
  [VP].
- Tests: whether you know what the thing is. Often the only text a busy reader sees.
- Structure: who it helps + what it does + the result, in one ABT line. Titles: specific and plain.
- Mistake: a clever title that hides the subject; a summary that starts with background.
- 25–50 words maximum.

**73. References and recommenders**
- Seen: a separate Gates Cambridge reference that ranks you against a named peer group, up to 4,000 characters [GC];
  three recommenders who submit through their own link, no family [SS]; three letters for Global Talent, each from an
  established expert who has known your work for 12 months, each with different examples, signed and dated, up to
  three A4 sides [GT]; two supporters on an official template [DIANA]; five to eight referees for Rhodes, four
  academic [RH].
- Tests: outside confirmation of the same story, with new detail.
- Structure (the brief you send): the programme and criteria → the two stories you want confirmed → facts with dates
  → the deadline and how to submit → a polite way to decline [PF][KZ].
- Mistake: famous names who barely know you [S-TED]; late asks (give three to four weeks [PF][KZ]); writing the letter
  for them without saying so (answer library rule); two letters repeating the same example [GT].
- Reminders: about a week before the deadline; ask them to check spam for the portal link [SS][KZ].

**74. CV / resume**
- Seen: resume up to two pages, Word or PDF [TRIN]; typed CV up to three A4 sides [GT]; resume of educational,
  professional, volunteer and organising experience [EG26]; professional resume for professional degrees, full CV for
  academic ones [S-FUL].
- Tests: fast verification. First screen is seconds [HRD].
- Structure: role, organisation, dates, one result per line; headings; single column.
- Mistake: CV facts that conflict with the essays; multi-column layouts that parsers and readers skip [HRD].
- Length: as the form says; otherwise two pages.

**75. Evidence documents, links and portfolios**
- Seen: up to 10 evidence documents, each up to three A4 sides, at least two for leadership or promise and four for

  working links to organisation, LinkedIn, press and awards [OYW]; work samples or portfolio link [WORK].
- Tests: proof behind claims.
- Structure: an evidence map: claim → document → page. Name files so the reviewer knows what each is.
- Mistake: dead links; links where the form says hyperlinks will be deleted [MRAC]; reusing one document for two
  criteria [GT].

**76. Video or audio response**
- Seen: one 60-second video, "Why do you do what you do?", face to camera only, no text, logos or effects; MOV or
  MP4, or a full YouTube link [EGV]; one-minute introduction, highly recommended, unlisted YouTube or Vimeo link,
  never password-protected [SS]; product demo, pitch deck and team introduction video [TS]; introductory video shot
  on a phone [ABH]; a video makes an interview statistically more likely [YC].
- Tests: the person behind the text. Echoing Green says it asks partly because of AI-written applications [EGV].
- Structure: name and work (10 seconds) → one moment (30) → why it matters and what you want (20). Outline, do not
  script word for word [DU].
- Mistake: going over time, even by seconds [COL]; reading from notes; adding graphics where banned [EGV]; dark,
  noisy rooms [PF-V].
- Spoken word rate: about 130–150 words per minute, so 60 seconds holds roughly 140 words.

**77. Pitch deck and demo**
- Seen: product demo video and pitch deck [TS]; demo checked by partners [YC]; evidence a novel technology works
  [SOLVE].
- Tests: the business at a glance.
- Structure: problem, solution, traction, model, market, team, ask; one idea per slide.
- Mistake: a deck that needs a presenter to make sense.

**78. Talk proposal: abstract, audience, takeaways**

- Structure: answer straight, with one specific detail; tie it back to the work in the last line.

- Tests: context that changes the reading.
- Structure: the gap or extra → the fact → why it matters to their criteria.
- Mistake: leaving it blank when there is a real gap to explain; using it to paste a second essay.

## 3. Form mechanics and pitfalls checklist

Run this on every form before drafting and again before hand-over.

### Limits and counting

- [ ] **Units.** Is the limit in words, characters with spaces, characters without spaces, lines or pages? Record
  each in the sheet. Echoing Green counts characters including spaces [EG20]. One Submittable funder counts letters,
  numbers, spaces and paragraph breaks [MRAC].
- [ ] **Paragraph breaks cost characters.** In fields that count them, each break is one or two characters. Fewer
  paragraphs, not more, when tight.
- [ ] **Special characters may cost more.** NIH notes that characters outside a standard English keyboard can count
  as more than one (seen via search; the NIH page refused the fetch). Use plain ASCII in tight fields.
- [ ] **Minimums exist.** UCAS needs at least 350 characters per answer inside a 4,000 total (via search) [S-UCAS].
- [ ] **Hard caps can disqualify.** Schwarzman says over-limit essays may be disqualified and that titles, headers and
  footnotes count [SS].
- [ ] **Count where it will be judged.** Word, Google Docs and web forms count differently (hyphens, dashes, numbers,
  URLs). Draft offline, then paste into the real field and read its counter. Keep 3–5% headroom for character fields.
- [ ] **Numeric fields.** Follow the format exactly. Echoing Green's amount field took digits and a period only [EG20].
  Currency, thousands separators and words fail validation.

### Text handling

- [ ] **Paste as plain text.** Word turns straight quotes into curly ones and dashes into special characters, which
  turn into garbage in some systems [CN]. Use Ctrl+Shift+V or paste through a plain-text editor.
- [ ] **Formatting will be stripped.** Assume no bold, italics, bullets or tables survive in a text box. Write so
  the answer reads without them. Use line breaks sparingly.
- [ ] **Hyperlinks may be deleted** and can affect eligibility where the panel judges only the form text [MRAC]. Put
  links only where a field asks for them.
- [ ] **Straight quotes and plain hyphens** in final text. Curly quotes are also on Wikipedia's list of surface signs
  of AI-drafted text [WIKI].
- [ ] **Language.** Many programmes accept English only (MWF, Solve, TED via search, Diana Award evidence)
  [MWF][SOLVE][DIANA]. Some publish guides in several languages: Anzisha in English, Arabic, French and Portuguese; TEF FAQs in the
  same four [ANZ][TEF]. If the applicant answers in a second language, the facts and the numbers from the profile must
  match the English sheet exactly.

### Saving, sessions and submission

- [ ] **Drafts may not save.** One Young World's form does not save progress; draft elsewhere and paste [OYW]. MWF
  saves but advises saving often [MWF].
- [ ] **Sessions time out.** Some grant portals end a session after 30 minutes idle with no warning and lose unsaved
  work (seen via search for WebGrants and state portals). Save each page; keep the master text in the master sheet.
- [ ] **Submission is final.** MWF does not allow changes after submit, by portal or email [MWF]. YC accepts material
  updates through an edit page [YCF]. Solve needed a separate "post" action before an application counted [SOLVE2].
  Find the real submit step.
- [ ] **Required pages versus scored pages.** Solve required only two pages to submit, yet reviewers read the rest
  [SOLVE2]. Treat every unlocked field as read.
- [ ] **One application is enough.** Duplicates do not raise chances and may count against [TS][F30].
- [ ] **Deadlines in the organiser's zone.** Chevening listed 11:00 UTC; TED used 23:59 UTC (via search) [CHVA]
  [S-TED]. Convert to the applicant's time zone in the sheet. Do not leave it to the last hour; talk reviewers say the same [OSC].
- [ ] **Fees.** Global Talent endorsement costs £561 before the visa fee [GTD]. Flag any fee at the top of the sheet.

### Attachments

- [ ] **File types and sizes.** Check each upload field. Submittable forms usually accept PDF, Word and images, with
  per-field limits set by the organiser, often 10–50 MB (seen via search). Default to PDF.
- [ ] **Page limits.** Global Talent: CV up to three A4 sides, each evidence item up to three sides, letters up to
  three sides excluding the author's credentials [GTD].
- [ ] **Naming.** `Surname_Firstname_<Programme>_<Item>_<yyyy-mm>.pdf`. No spaces or special characters. One item per
  file unless the form asks to merge.
- [ ] **Scans and stamps.** Submit the version with watermarks or seals where they exist; copies without them can
  raise authenticity questions [O1].
- [ ] **Translations.** A full English translation with a translator's certificate of completeness and competence; a
  summary is not accepted [USCIS]. The certificate names the document, the languages, the translator, the date and a
  signature [ATA].
- [ ] **Video.** Length, orientation, file type, host and privacy setting as instructed. Unlisted, not private or
  password-protected [SS]. Full URL, not a shortened or mobile link [EGV].

### Optional, demographic and consent fields

- [ ] **"Optional" usually means scored if answered.** A recommended video makes interview more likely [YC][SS]. An
  optional notes-to-reviewers field is where the reviewer learns the structure [PYCON]. Leave a field blank only when
  there is nothing true to say.
- [ ] **Demographic fields are the applicant's alone.** Voluntary, often with a decline option, often kept from the decision
  makers [S-CC305]. Race, religion, health, sexual orientation are special-category data [ICO-S]. The desk never fills
  them by inference.
- [ ] **Consent boxes.** Read each. Valid consent is a clear opt-in, specific and unbundled; pre-ticked boxes do not
  count [ICO-C]. Note any field that will be published (bios, short descriptions) [TRIN][MRAC].
- [ ] **Declarations.** Own-work certificates, AI disclosures, conflict-of-interest forms and truth statements are
  signed by the applicant only after they have read the whole application [RH][SS][MER].

### Referees

- [ ] Ask three to four weeks ahead, with a polite way to decline [PF][KZ].
- [ ] Send the brief: programme, criteria, two stories to confirm, CV, deadline, submission route [KZ].
- [ ] Track each referee: asked, accepted, link sent, submitted. Remind about a week before [KZ]; ask them to check
  spam [SS].
- [ ] Letters must be written for this application only where the rules say so [GT]. Never send the same letter text
  to two programmes that could compare.

## 4. AI use and originality

### Policy patterns (what programmes say)

| Pattern | Examples | What the desk does |
|---|---|---|
| **Forbids** AI-written answers | Chevening (own original work; AI content treated as plagiarism and fraud) [CHVF]; One Young World (do not use AI to write answers) [OYW]; Schwarzman (certify no AI generator was used) [SS]; NIH (applications substantially developed by AI are not treated as original; detection tools used) [CITI][REG] | Prepare the record, the structure and the checklist of what each question tests. Boxes stay empty. The applicant writes. |
| **Discourages, judges by authenticity** | Echoing Green welcomes thoughtful use for language and access barriers but may rank mostly-AI applications lower or rule them out [EG26]; Trinity strongly discourages AI essays [TRIN] | Draft only from the applicant's words and record; they rewrite openings and closings themselves. |
| **Disclose** | MWF (disclose any technology used, including AI; you stay responsible) [MWF]; UKRI (declare substantive use; minor language help exempt) [UKRI]; Wellcome, RGS (declare) [THS][RGS]; NSF (encouraged) [SRAI] | Carry the disclosure line in the sheet; make it true. |
| **Allows, author accountable** | ERC (brainstorming, revision, translation allowed; full authorship responsibility) [THS]; Canadian agencies (drafting allowed with disclosure) [SRAI] | Normal desk process plus the "Make it yours" pass. |
| **Silent** | Many foundations and competitions | Read the FAQ and terms; record "silent" with the page checked. |

Two further rules recur. **No AI in interviews** that are part of the application (UKRI) [UKRI]. **Reviewers may not
use AI** on applications at NIH, UKRI, ERC and NSF, mainly for confidentiality [THS][SRAI]; but UK funders announced
in June 2026 that they may use AI to process applications, with humans deciding [RS]. Some funders also cap volume:
NIH limits each lead investigator to six applications a year, a response to floods of AI-assisted submissions
[CITI][REG].

### Detection: facts

- **Detectors are weak.** OpenAI withdrew its own classifier in July 2023: it caught 26% of AI text, flagged 9% of
  human text, and was unreliable under 1,000 characters [DEC]. A test of 14 tools including Turnitin found them
  neither accurate nor reliable, and obfuscation made them worse [WW].
- **Vendors admit error rates.** Turnitin reports under 1% false positives at document level (for documents over 20%
  AI) and about 4% at sentence level, more often in mixed documents and next to AI passages [TII]. It raised its
  minimum length from 150 to 300 words, marks scores under 20% as less reliable, and found extra false positives in
  the first and last sentences [CT].
- **Non-native writers are flagged more.** Seven detectors labelled 61% of TOEFL essays by non-native writers as AI,
  while scoring US eighth-graders almost perfectly; at least one detector flagged 89 of 91 [KM][THE][LIANG]. A 2026
  study of 135,389 manuscripts found false-positive rates from 0% to 100% across 13 detectors, moved by professional
  editing style alone [PARK]. For applicants who write in Nigerian or another regional English, heavy polishing can raise scores,
  not lower them.
- **Humans are not much better.** Wikipedia's editors say people perform little better than chance and that tools are
  of little use; they look instead for habits [WIKI][TC].
- **Similarity checks are separate and reliable.** Copying from websites, brochures or other applications is caught
  as plagiarism; CSC names it among the reasons for rejection [CSC][CSCFB]. Funders also compare across a round.

### Detection: myths

- *"A detector score proves AI use."* No. Vendors themselves say to treat flags as a reason for a conversation about
  process [TII].
- *"Swap the tell-tale words and it passes."* Word lists have weak evidence; the habits (inflated importance, vague
  attribution, tidy summaries) persist [TC]. Wikipedia warns that fixing surface signs misses the real problem [WIKI].
- *"Polished English is safer."* Editing style alone can push scores up [PARK].
- *"Short answers are safe."* Detection is unreliable on short text in both directions [DEC]; reviewers judge
  generic short answers as less competitive anyway [TRIN].
- *"If AI is allowed, anything goes."* Every policy keeps the applicant responsible for accuracy; fabricated
  citations and invented facts count as misconduct [REG][UKRI].

### Keeping answers human (and true)

What reviewers and detectors both react to is text no person would say that way. The signs Wikipedia's editors
collected are a checklist of what to remove [WIKI]:

1. **Inflated significance.** Cut "pivotal", "plays a vital role", "a testament to". State the fact.
2. **Trailing -ing clauses** that add vague meaning ("…, highlighting the importance of access"). End on the fact.
3. **Vague attribution** ("experts say", "studies show"). Name the source or cut the claim.
4. **Promotional tone.** Write like a report, not an advert.
5. **"Serves as" instead of "is".** Use the plain verb.
6. **Not X but Y** correcting a claim nobody made. Delete the X.
7. **Habitual threes.** Lists of exactly three by reflex. Use the true number.
8. **Summary closers** ("In conclusion", "Overall"). End on the last real point or the ask.
9. **Formatting tells.** Title-case headings, bold everywhere, emoji, curly quotes in a plain form.
10. **Banned words** from the house list and the applicant's own voice rules in `desk/voice.md`.

Then add what only the applicant has: a name, a date, a place, a number from their records, a thing they saw, a
decision they made and why, a doubt they had. Keep the draft history (the master sheet and its version history): if anyone questions authorship,
process is the answer, and that is what Turnitin itself recommends asking about [TII]. Finish with the skill's "Make
it yours" pass and `scripts/originality.py`.

### The ethics line

- Never inflate: a shortlist is not a win, a pilot is not a programme, reach is not outcome, a pledge is not money.
- Never invent: no quotes, people, partners, figures, dates or letters that do not exist. Mark gaps `[TBC]`.
- Never claim a policy, registration or approval that is not in place; say what will exist and by when.
- Never write a referee's letter for signature without saying so; never send identical letters to comparable
  programmes.
- Never certify what is not true, including AI-use statements. If the desk drafted, the form's honest answer
  reflects that, or the applicant writes the answer themselves.
- Expect checks: fact-checkers ask for tax returns and references [VP]; due diligence follows shortlisting [S-ABH].
  One false line can end the record with that funder and its network.

## 5. The universal fill procedure

For any unfamiliar application, from first read to "ready for the applicant to submit". It sits inside the skill's workflow
(Scout → Fill → Hand over) and adds the form-reading steps.

**Step 1. Get the real form.** Find the official page, the guide, the FAQ, the terms and, if published, an offline
worksheet or PDF of the questions (Echoing Green and Solve publish them [EG26][SOLVE2]). If the questions are only
visible after login, ask the applicant to open an account and export or screenshot the questions; the desk never creates
accounts or signs in. Save fetched guidance text to the application folder for `originality.py --against`.

**Step 2. Gate.** List every hard requirement: age, nationality, residency, stage, registration, revenue, language,
team, referees available, fees, travel dates. Fail one and stop: Ineligible, with the reason, in the tracker.

**Step 3. Find the scoring.** Look for criteria, rubric, weightings and stages (screening, scoring, interview, due
diligence). Copy each criterion into the sheet header in the programme's own words. If there is no rubric, infer one
from the question order and the "what we look for" text.

**Step 4. Record the mechanics.** For each field: number, exact wording, type (essay, short text, number, select,
upload, video, declaration), limit and unit, required or optional, published or private. Add time zone, fee,
AI policy (forbids, disclose, checks, silent) with the page it came from, and how to submit.

**Step 5. Map questions to the taxonomy.** Tag each field with its type number from section 2. Two fields with the
same type (a leadership essay and a leadership short answer) get different examples. Note which questions a
referee, a video or an attachment will also cover, so the set tells one story with no repeats (UCAS reads its
answers together for the same reason [S-UCAS]).

**Step 6. Build the evidence map.** For each criterion: the story from `desk/story-bank.md`, the facts from
`desk/profile.md`, the document from `desk/evidence.md`, and the gap as `[TBC]`. Decide the protagonist per question (the
applicant, the team, or the people served).

**Step 7. Decide who writes.** If the policy forbids AI-written answers, stop drafting: give the applicant the checklist per
question (what it tests, the structure, the stories and facts to use). Otherwise continue.

**Step 8. Draft.** First sentence answers the question. Follow the structure for the type. Use the criterion's
words once where they fit. Write at about 90–100% of the limit. Keep numbers to the verified figures in the profile.

**Step 9. Cut and count.** Count in the unit the form uses, then in the live field if the applicant can paste a test.
Leave headroom for characters. Replace curly quotes and special dashes in tight fields.

**Step 10. Check against section 4.** Remove the ten AI habits. Run `scripts/originality.py` against other sheets
and the saved funder text. Banned-word scan.

**Step 11. Compliance pass.** Every field present, in order, within limit; numbers formatted; declarations listed
for the applicant to sign; demographics and consents left for the applicant with a note on each; attachments named and within page
limits; translations certified; links working and allowed; video plan within time.

**Step 12. Referees and third parties.** Briefs sent by the applicant, tracker in the sheet, reminder dates set.

**Step 13. Assess.** "Before you submit": what carries it, what stands against it, the one email to send if a rule is
unclear, the recommendation (send, send after fixes, skip), and the "Make it yours" pass.

**Step 14. Hand over.** Final copy, tracker row, `[TBC]` list grouped so the applicant answers each fact once.
The applicant pastes, uploads, signs and submits. Status becomes Submitted only on their word.
