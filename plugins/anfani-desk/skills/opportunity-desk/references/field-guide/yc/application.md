# YC application: the form, question by question

Researched October 2026. Deadlines, rules and policies change: check each programme's own page before acting.

Research of 17 October 2026 for the Winter 2027 (W27) application. Every claim comes from a page in the
sources list at the end. Facts about the company and the founders come only from the applicant's profile file
`desk/profile.md`; anything only the founders know is `[TBC]`.

**Key dates.** On-time deadline 2 November 2026, 8pm PT (04:00 in Lagos, 3 November). Interviews mostly by video
in November and December. Decision for on-time applicants by 11 December 2026. Batch January to March 2027,
in person in San Francisco, opening with a three-day kick-off. Standard deal $500k. (YC apply page.)

## 1. What YC says gets people in

These come from YC's own pages and partner talks. They decide more than any single answer.

1. **Clarity first.** Paul Graham: of the groups good enough to interview, more than half lose because the
   application does not show it. A reader handles about 100 applications a day, so the point goes in the
   first sentence. (How to Apply)
2. **The founders matter more than the idea.** Graham reads "what is your company going to make" first to have
   something to hang the rest on, then spends most care on the founders. He calls the "something impressive
   each founder has built" question the most important on the form. (How to Apply)
3. **Technical talent is the biggest single variable for an interview.** Dalton Caldwell (2023): teams where at
   least one founder could be hired into a technical role at a top YC company have about five times better odds
   of an interview. Non-technical teams on an unlaunched tarpit idea have the lowest odds. (Caldwell, 2023)
4. **Follow the instructions.** Caldwell says YC scores whether you followed the directions, because it shows
   attention to detail. Fill every field, check grammar, make the video exactly as asked. (Caldwell, 2018 and 2023)
5. **Tell a story the reader can repeat.** Caldwell builds a short story in his head: who the founders are, how
   they met, who does what, what exists today, whether anyone uses it, whether they are serious, and what is
   unusual. If he can tell that story, the application worked. (Caldwell, 2023)
6. **Seriousness is a signal.** Leaving a job to work full time is evidence. So is applying again with more
   progress each time. (Caldwell, 2023)
7. **Honesty is a hard gate.** Caldwell: any sign of deliberate misstatement of revenue, traction, background or
   education leads to rejection. Presenting monthly revenue that is really annual, or a founder's salary as
   company revenue, are the examples he names. Extraordinary claims need extraordinary evidence. (Caldwell, 2023)
8. **Name the obstacles yourself.** Graham: disclose the flaws. If partners find a problem you did not mention,
   they assume you never saw it. (How to Apply)
9. **A demo beats a plan.** Stephanie Simon (YC): a rough demo counts, even one running locally. The gap between
   nothing and something is large. (YC Library)
10. **Video submitters are interviewed more often.** Graham says this outright; YC's video page repeats the rules.
    (How to Apply; Video page)
11. **Plain English.** Garry Tan: no buzzwords, put the conclusion first and the proof after it (the "inverted
    pyramid"). Show a specific user with a specific problem; say if anyone pays. (Tan, tips video)
12. **The market must be able to get large.** Tan: is there a real way to $100M a year in revenue in five to ten
    years? (Tan, tips video)

**What makes applications fail** (Caldwell, Graham, Tan, Leland, Arc):
- Fields left blank, no URLs, no founder bios, sloppy writing.
- A video that breaks the rules: too long, a missing co-founder, a product demo, music.
- Jargon and marketing language; a reader cannot say what the company does.
- It is unclear who builds the product, or code was written by non-founders and not explained.
- "Well designed and easy to use" offered as the insight. Graham calls that a claim to execute, not an insight.
- "No competitors."
- Obstacles the partners can see but the founders do not mention.
- Inflated or blurred numbers. This is disqualifying, not just weak.

**Late, reapplying, referrals.**
- **Late:** YC keeps reading after the deadline and funds many late applicants each batch. Most late
  applications get a decision within a month, with no promised date. YC will not mark a late application as on
  time. Caldwell (2018) said odds are slightly lower early or late because it is not the main reading window.
  Apply on time. (FAQ; Caldwell, 2018)
- **Reapplying:** about half of a typical batch applied more than once before acceptance. Progress since the
  last application is a strong signal. One application per batch. Past applications can be referenced, but a
  new form is needed. (FAQ) Interviewees who are turned down get emailed feedback, usually on one main concern;
  showing you fixed or disproved it next time is weighed heavily. (Interview guide; Caldwell, 2023)
- **Referrals and alumni:** YC says it considers all applications equally and does not rely on introductions.
  Caldwell: it is basically impossible to network your way in; most people funded are complete strangers. Be
  wary of anyone offering to "get you in" for equity or money. Recommendations from a previous application carry
  over. The form does ask who encouraged you and which YC events you attended, so an alumni encouragement is
  worth naming honestly, but it is not a lever. (FAQ; Caldwell, 2018 and 2023)

**Two small new items (2026).**
- **Paxel builder report** (optional). YC's FAQ: link a Paxel account to your YC account and opt in on the
  application, and YC considers the report; if not, the application is judged exactly as before. Paxel reads
  local AI coding sessions (Claude Code, Codex CLI, Cursor) and scores how you build. It uploads derived data
  such as prompt excerpts and file paths, so check what it sends before opting in. For two technical founders
  who build with AI daily, this is a cheap extra signal. (FAQ; Paxel coverage)
- **Tech stack question** now asks for AI models and AI coding tools used. (Round Funded 2026 reconstruction)

## 2. The form, in order

**How sure we are of the wording.** YC does not publish the full form. The live form is at
apply.ycombinator.com and changes between batches; it also branches (a "No" to "Do you have revenue?" hides the
revenue questions). Labels used below:

- **[YC-published]** wording printed on a YC page.
- **[2024–26 copy]** wording reproduced by an applicant or a 2026 reconstruction (Round Funded 2026; Gus Rasch's
  submitted application; Nick Raushenbush's 2024–25 guide).
- **[Older form]** wording from Shizune's collection or older applications; may be retired or moved into the
  founder profile.
- **[Inferred]** not seen verbatim; inferred from guides.

Limits: YC publishes only one hard limit, 50 characters. Everything else is a free text box. Lengths below are
the desk's targets, drawn from the guides cited.

### 2.1 Founders section

**Q1. "Who writes code, or does other technical work on your product? Was any of it done by a non-founder?
Please explain."** [2024–26 copy]
- Looks for: that the founders can build it themselves. YC's FAQ says the team must have the skills to build the
  product rather than outsource it. Caldwell's top tip is to make crystal clear who builds. (FAQ; Caldwell)
- Structure: one sentence on who built what. One or two sentences on any non-founder work: who, under what
  contract, and that the company owns the code.
- Mistakes: vagueness about who built it; hiding contractor or staff work.
- If the product grew inside an existing company (for example a services agency the founders run) and its staff
  wrote code, say so, say they were that company's employees, and say where the IP sits and that it will move to
  the new company `[TBC: who wrote which parts]`. Never write "only founders" unless it is true.

**Q2. "Are you looking for a cofounder?"** [2024–26 copy] Usually shown to solo founders. Answer "No" with one
line if both founders are full partners.

**Founder profile (each founder fills in their own).** The profile is filled through each founder's YC account
after the lead founder adds them by email. Wording below is [Older form] unless marked.

**Q3. "Please tell us in one or two sentences about something impressive that each founder has built or
achieved."** [YC-published, How to Apply]
- Looks for: magnitude, not type. Graham: the hardest thing you have done, preferably intellectually; it need
  not be resume material. Do not list the startup itself; YC already knows about it. Generic praise ("dedicated,
  gives 100%") carries no weight. (How to Apply)
- Structure: the thing, the hard part, the result, a date. One or two sentences per founder.
- Pattern (paraphrased): "Built X alone in N weeks; it now does Y for Z people." "Won a place on a selective list
  of N from M applicants."
- Pick candidates from the verified record in `desk/profile.md`. Pick the hardest build, not the longest list:
  one achievement per founder, stated plainly.

**Q4. "Please tell us about the time you most successfully hacked some (non-computer) system to your
advantage."** [YC-published, How to Apply]
- Looks for: people who beat systems, not obedient people. Graham says a strong answer makes him re-read an
  application he was about to pass, and some groups were interviewed mainly on it. The question came from Sam
  Altman. (How to Apply; What We Look for in Founders)
- Structure: the system and its rule → what you noticed → what you did → the result. Three to five sentences.
- Mistakes: a computer hack; breaking rules that matter; an invented story.
- Use a true story from each founder `[TBC]`; never one the founder has ruled out.

**Q5. "How long have the founders known one another and how did you meet? Have any of the founders not met in
person?"** [Older form; still in 2024–25 guides]
- Looks for: founder durability. Graham lists friendship and a strong founder relationship among his five
  founder qualities. (What We Look for in Founders)
- Structure: years known → how you met → what you have shipped together → confirm you have met in person.
- If the founders now live in different countries, say how you work across them `[TBC: how long apart, how you
  work]`.

**Q6. "Please tell us about an interesting project, preferably outside of class or work, that two or more of you
created together."** [Older form] Harj Taggar has said partners valued learning what applicants build in their
spare time. Use a real joint project with a link.

**Founder profile fields seen in guides:** education, work history, whether you are technical (answer yes only
if you can build the product without outside help; Raushenbush), LinkedIn and GitHub, and an equity percentage.
Raushenbush quotes the form's note that founders must hold at least 10% each. [2024–26 copy]

### 2.2 Founder video

**Q7. "Please record a one minute video introducing the founder(s)."** [2024–26 copy; older wording asked for a
1-minute unlisted, not private, YouTube URL]

YC's rules (Video page):
- One minute.
- Every founder appears. If you cannot be together, record a video call.
- Only the founders talking. No other voices or footage.
- Say who you are, what you are building and why.
- Speak from bullet points, not a script.
- Not a demo or promo video. Demos go elsewhere.

What partners have added:
- Caldwell: people break these rules all the time (20-minute "spirit journey" videos, a demo with no humans,
  a missing co-founder). A missing co-founder can read as a co-founder who does not know you applied. If you
  can, wait a few days and film together. (Caldwell, 2018)
- Michael Seibel (Lagos, via Chingu): he plays the video first; a soundtrack signals time spent on the video
  instead of the business.
- Gillian O'Brien (Cherry, W19, via Pilot): partners watch how the founders relate; be at ease and do not talk
  over each other.
- For a team split across countries: a recorded video call is allowed. Film together only if a real chance
  arises before the deadline; do not delay submission for it.

Shape (about 60 seconds): each founder's name and role (20 s) → the 50-character line and who it is
for (10 s) → why us, one fact each (15 s) → what exists now and one honest number (15 s). Captions help silent
viewing, but no music, effects or b-roll.

### 2.3 Company section

**Q8. "Company name"** [2024–26 copy] The current name.

**Q9. "Describe what your company does in 50 characters or less."** [2024–26 copy; YC-enforced limit]
- Looks for: a phrase a stranger can repeat. Raushenbush suggests writing about 25 versions; "X for Y" only if X
  is famous. Causo calls it the hardest field.
- Kevin Hale's examples praise lines that set up the reader's questions without explaining how.
- Write a dozen drafts, count characters on each, and let the founders choose.

**Q10. "Company URL, if any"** [2024–26 copy]

**Q11. "Please provide a link to the product, if any."** [2024–26 copy] Older wording: "If you have a demo,
what's the url?" Give the live web app and any app-store link. If a login is needed, give test credentials
created for reviewers. Raushenbush: a 60–90 second demo video works if the product is not public. Simon: rough
is fine.

**Q12. "What is your company going to make? Please describe your product and what it does or will do."**
[2024–26 copy; YC-published in older form]
- Looks for: a matter-of-fact description close enough that the reader could reproduce it. Graham's test: after
  reading, how close is the reader to rebuilding it? Narrow and concrete beats grand and empty. A comparison to
  something known is efficient. (How to Apply)
- Structure (Raushenbush; Tan): what it is and what it does today (1–2 sentences) → one proof fact → what it
  becomes (1 sentence). Two to four sentences.
- Mistakes: opening with how important the problem is; "platform"; "AI-powered" as the description.
- Pattern (paraphrased): "[Name] does [what] for [whom]: [the core action in plain words]. Live on [platform]
  since [month year]."

**Q13. "Where do you live now, and where would the company be based after YC?"** [2024–26 copy]
**Q14. "Explain your decision regarding location."** [2024–26 copy; seen in Gus Rasch's 2025 form]
- What YC says: the batch is in person in San Francisco for three months; after that "you can go wherever you
  want". YC funds companies from every country. (FAQ; Caldwell, 2023) Basedash's founder notes YC does not
  require a move to the Bay Area after the batch.
- What it tests: commitment to attend, and whether the location choice is reasoned (where users, hiring and
  investors are).
- Co-founders in different countries: YC has no published rule against it. Its co-founder guide asks teams to
  agree where the company is based, where each lives and whether they work in person or remotely. YC's matching
  data (2021) showed 55% of matches in the same country. Partners in interviews ask about team dynamics; one
  2025 interviewee reports questions on a co-founder's time commitment. (YC co-founder questions; TechCrunch
  2021; Gyfted)
- Honest pattern: "[Founder A] lives in [city]; [Founder B] lives in [city]. Both will be in San Francisco for
  the batch `[subject to visas, see deal-and-logistics.md]`. After YC the company will be based in [place] because
  our users are in [market] and [reason]." State how the founders work today across any time difference.
- Older forms asked which founders would live in the Bay Area for the batch. A plan that leaves a founder out
  reads as weak commitment (Zain Shah, S13). Do not promise attendance you cannot deliver; see the visa section
  in `deal-and-logistics.md` before writing this answer.

### 2.4 Progress section

**Q15. "How far along are you?"** [2024–26 copy]
- Looks for: the state of the product today and how fast you move. Caldwell: what exactly exists right now? Is it
  built? Are there users?
- Structure: launched when → what is live → users with definitions → the latest change. Two to five sentences,
  facts not hopes (Raushenbush).
- Give launch dates per platform and the exact user count with its definition (for example registered
  accounts) and the export date `[TBC]`.

**Q16. "How long have each of you been working on this? How much of that has been full-time? Please explain."**
[2024–26 copy] See section 3 on full-time commitments. Give dates per founder, and when each went or will go
full-time.

**Q17. "What tech stack are you using, or planning to use, to build this product? Include AI models and AI
coding tools you use."** [2024–26 copy; AI clause per Round Funded 2026]
- One applicant paired the stack with usage evidence (Rohan Balkondekar, F24). Name the real stack: front end,
  back end, database and hosting `[TBC: what may be said publicly]`, the AI models used in the product, and the
  coding tools (Claude Code and others). Two sentences.

**Q18. "Are people using your product?"** Yes/No. [2024–26 copy]

**Q19. If yes: "How many active users or customers do you have? How many are paying? Who is paying you the most,
and how much do they pay you?"** [Older form; still in 2024–25 guides]
**If no: "When will you have a version people can use?"** [2024–26 copy]
- How to report honestly (YC interview guide; Caldwell; Graham, Startup = Growth; Raushenbush):
  - Active means used in a defined window (daily or weekly). Registered accounts are not active users. Give both,
    each with its definition and date.
  - Growth is measured weekly. Graham: during YC 5–7% a week is good, 10% exceptional, 1% a sign you have not
    found it yet; revenue is the best measure, active users the next best for products that do not charge.
  - YC may ask you to verify any number stated in the interview. Keep the export.
  - Paying: zero is an acceptable answer. "0 paying; we have not charged yet" is better than any blur.
- Pattern: "[N] registered accounts since [date]. [M] active in the last 30 days (active = opened a match or set a
  reminder). Weekly actives grew from [a] to [b] between [month] and [month]. No paying users yet."
- Mistakes: "1,000+ users" with no definition; cumulative counts dressed as growth; launch spikes as traction.
  The traction rules in `../accelerators.md` section 3 apply in full.

**Q20. "Do you have revenue?"** Yes/No. [2024–26 copy]
- If yes, YC asks for revenue by month for recent months, "not cumulative and not GMV" [Older form wording], and
  "Where does your revenue come from? If your revenue comes from multiple sources (ex. multiple products,
  multiple companies or a mix of consulting and this product), please break down how much is coming from each
  source." [2024–25 copy, Raushenbush]
- **If a parent or sister company earns client revenue,** that is not the startup's revenue. If the product itself
  has no revenue, answer "No". If the form or interview asks about the parent company, say plainly that it is a
  services firm with client income, and give the split `[TBC]`. Caldwell names salary-as-revenue as a typical
  misstatement; agency income as product revenue is the same mistake.

**Q21. "Anything else you would like us to know regarding your revenue or growth rate?"** [Older form] Use for
one clarification only (for example, how users arrived, or a dated change).

**Q22. "If you are applying with the same idea as a previous batch, did anything change? If you applied with a
different idea, why did you pivot and what did you learn from the last idea?"** [2024–26 copy]
- First application: one line saying so `[TBC: has either founder applied before?]`.
- If reapplying: lead with measurable progress since last time (Raushenbush; Caldwell). If pivoting: spend the
  words on what you learned.

**Q23. "If you have already participated or committed to participate in an incubator, "accelerator" or
"pre-accelerator" program, please tell us about it."** [2024–26 copy]
- YC: doing another accelerator is allowed, but YC may then expect more progress. (FAQ)
- Building a programme's software, or speaking at its sessions, is not participation. Workspace vouchers are not an
  accelerator. Name each programme the company actually joined, what was given and what equity was taken (none, if
  none).

### 2.5 Idea section

**Q24. "Why did you pick this idea to work on? Do you have domain expertise in this area? How do you know people
need what you're making?"** [2024–26 copy]
- Looks for: a personal connection and evidence of need from real people. Kevin Hale: the idea is a hypothesis
  made of a problem, a solution and an insight. Generic market-size answers read as an outsider (ycinsight).
- Structure: why you (one sentence, true) → domain expertise (one sentence) → evidence of need, primary data
  first (usage, retention, user words), then supporting data. Four to five sentences (Raushenbush).
- Keep the founder's own connection to one sentence and tell it from strength, not hardship. Evidence:
  `[TBC: usage and retention figures; one named user outcome, with consent]`.

**Q25. "What's new about what you're making? What substitutes do people resort to because it doesn't exist yet
(or they don't know about it)?"** [Older form; may be merged into Q26 now]
- Looks for: the "before" state and the specific difference. Paystack (W16) named the substitute exactly: firms
  set up US or UK entities to use foreign processors.
- Name the substitutes exactly (for example forwarded messages, screenshots, notice boards, generic list sites).

**Q26. "Who are your competitors? What do you understand about your business that they don't?"** [2024–26 copy]
- Looks for: knowledge of the market and an insight. Graham: "better designed and easier" is not an insight;
  say exactly what you will do and why that is enough. YC's interview guide: know the existing products and what
  is wrong with them, and explain how you are different. Saying "none" signals you have not looked.
- Structure (Raushenbush): incumbents → the old way or patchwork → other startups → for each, where they miss.
  Three to five sentences.
- Tarpit check. Dalton Caldwell and Michael Seibel (2024): tarpit ideas are ones many founders keep trying,
  friends praise, and that keep failing while nothing has changed. Job boards and recommendation apps are often
  named as such in community threads. Seibel says he is now open to old tarpit ideas if the founder explains why
  new technology makes it work now, and he dislikes founders who ignore past attempts. So: name the products
  that came before, say why they stalled, and say what has changed now (for example what LLMs make possible at
  scale). That is the insight to state. `[TBC: the founders confirm the competitor they respect most]`.

**Q27. "How do or will you make money? How much could you make? (We realize you can't know precisely, but give
your best estimate.)"** [2024–26 copy; the bracket is reported by Gus Rasch and Raushenbush]
- Looks for: a buyer and a price, and the reasoning. Tan: is there a path to $100M a year in five to ten years?
  Raushenbush: show a bottom-up calculation (number of customers × annual price). Gus Rasch did bottom-up for
  realism and top-down for upside.
- If no model is confirmed, pick one to defend, show the arithmetic with sourced counts, and say what experiment
  tests it and when `[TBC]`. Never carry forward unverified claims from older decks or drafts.

**Q28. "How do users find your product? How did you get the users you have now? If you run paid ads, what is
your cost of acquisition?"** [Older form; still in 2024–25 guides]
- Structure: channels (one sentence) → the split of current users by source, in numbers → CAC if any paid.
- Separate users who came through the founders' own networks, non-profits or events from those who arrived on
  their own `[TBC: split]`. Affiliated users are real but partners will ask whether growth repeats.

**Q29. "If you track metrics around user engagement and retention, what are they?"** [2024–25 copy, Raushenbush]
Top two or three engagement metrics with values, and one retention figure with its method.

**Q30. "Where will most of your initial users be located?"** and **"Which category best applies to your
company?"** [2024–25 copy] Dropdowns. Pick the true market and the one category that fits best.

**Q31. "If you had any other ideas you considered applying with, please list them. One may be something we've
been waiting for. Often when we fund people it's to do something they list here and not in the main
application."** [Older form; reproduced in 2024–25 copies]
- Not a loyalty test (Raushenbush). Graham: YC often funds groups to work on an alternate idea when the founders
  are strong. Onu's founders were told in their interview that YC liked them but not the idea. (How to Apply;
  POCIT)
- Structure: up to three ideas, each in 50 characters plus one line on why. Only real ideas you would work on.
  Candidates from the founders' own work `[TBC: the founders choose]`.

### 2.6 Equity section

**Q32. "Have you formed ANY legal entity yet?"** [2024–26 copy]
**Q33. "Please list all legal entities you have and in what state or country each was formed (e.g. Delaware C
Corp, Mexican SAPI, Singapore Pvt Ltd, etc.)."** [2024–26 copy] The form's helper text (Raushenbush) asks to
include subsidiaries, entities formed in other markets, entities created before a pivot, and entities set up to
employ developers or other service providers.
- If the product sits inside an existing company, list that company. A separate non-profit the founders run is not
  a company entity of the startup, but disclose it under other commitments. Paystack (W16) answered the entity questions plainly and also answered
  whether any founder was covered by IP agreements and whether any code was written by a contractor.
- YC does not need you to incorporate in the US before applying. If accepted, a company incorporated outside the
  US, Canada, Singapore or the Cayman Islands creates a parent in one of them. (FAQ; Deal page) See
  `deal-and-logistics.md` for a product held inside an existing company.

**Q34. "Please describe the breakdown of the equity ownership in percentages among the founders, employees and
any other stockholders. If there are multiple founders, be sure to give the equity ownership of each founder and
founder title (e.g. CEO)."** [Older form; 2024–25 copies] If not formed, the form asks for the planned split and
notes the question is as much for you as for YC (Raushenbush).
- Caldwell: many teams have never discussed the split until the form asks; partners expect founders to know it
  in the interview. Raushenbush: model dilution; each founder must hold at least 10%.
- If the product sits inside an existing company, give that company's shareholding and the planned split of the
  new company `[TBC: both]`. If it has other shareholders, say so.

**Q35. "Have you taken any investment yet?"** [2024–26 copy] Grants and prizes are not equity investment; say
which is which. If yes, list investor, amount, valuation or cap, instrument and date.

**Q36. Money questions** [Older form; 2024–25 copies]: "How much money do you spend per month?", "How much
money does your company have in the bank now?", "How long is your runway?" Answer for the startup, not for any parent
company, or make the split explicit `[TBC]`.

**Q37. "Are you currently fundraising?"** [2024–26 copy] If yes, give start date, target, amount committed, and
how YC changes the plan. YC's FAQ: if accepted while fundraising, YC starts its investment at once.

**Q38. "Is there anything else we should know about your company?"** [Older form] Use it for the one material
fact not asked elsewhere: the founders' other commitments and the plan to go full-time (section 3), or the visa
plan. One short paragraph.

### 2.7 Curious section

**Q39. "What convinced you to apply to Y Combinator? Did someone encourage you to apply? Have you been to any YC
events?"** [2024–26 copy; Shizune] Name the reason in one line, the person if any, and events attended (Startup
School, YC talks). "No one" and "none" are fine answers.

**Q40. "How did you hear about Y Combinator?"** [2024–26 copy] One line, the true source.

### 2.8 Batch and extras

**Q41. "What batch do you want to apply for?"** [2024–26 copy] Choose the next regular batch. The other option ("A batch
after Winter 2027") is Early Decision, built for students. (Early Decision page)

**Q42. Paxel builder report opt-in.** [YC-published, FAQ] Optional. See section 1.

**Q43. "Please tell us something surprising or amusing that one of you has discovered."** [Older form] May be
retired. If shown: a true discovery from talking to users, specific (ycinsight).

**Not on the form, by YC's own statement:** a pitch deck. Caldwell (2023): no warm intros, no decks, no calls.
The form, video and demo are the whole submission.

## 3. Full-time and other commitments: the honest answer

**YC's rule.** You may apply while employed or studying. If accepted, founders commit to work full time on the
company during the batch and after it. (FAQ) The first use of the YC money is usually founder salaries, so
founders can stop other paid work. (FAQ)

**What partners read into it.** Caldwell (2023) counts leaving other work as evidence of seriousness. A 2025
interviewee reports partners questioning a co-founder's part-time faculty role. The founding questions YC
publishes for co-founders include "what needs to happen for each of us to go full-time". (Caldwell; Gyfted; YC
co-founder questions)

**The common situation.** Founders often run other things when they apply: a services firm with staff and client
revenue, a non-profit, an event, contract roles. YC expects every founder to commit full time from the batch.

**How to answer, in the form** (Q16 and Q38):
1. State the present truthfully, with numbers: hours per week each founder spends on the startup now `[TBC]`, and
   what else each does. Do not round part-time up to full-time.
2. State the commitment as a date and a mechanism: from [date], every founder works only on the startup. Any other
   company's client work is led by `[TBC: named person and role]`; no founder bills client hours. Any non-profit
   role moves to `[TBC: board-level only / delegated to named lead]`, with a stated weekly cap if any role remains.
3. Separate the money. The startup's revenue is its own; a parent company's income is not. If another company pays
   the founders now, say when that stops.
4. Name the one risk and the fix. Partners prefer candour about obstacles to dismissal (Interview guide; Graham).

**Paraphrased pattern:** "Today [Founder A] spends about [X] hours a week on [startup] and also runs [firm], the
software firm that built it, and [non-profit]. [Founder B] spends [Y]. From [date], both of us work on [startup]
full-time; [firm]'s client work moves to [name], and [Founder A]'s non-profit role becomes a board seat."

**In the interview:** expect "Is everyone full-time?" and "What happens to the agency?" Answer in two sentences
with the date and the name. Caldwell says partners want founders who know their own business; this is part of it.

**What not to do:** describe the agency as a side project if it is not; promise to close the other company unless that is
the plan; let one founder answer for the other's commitments.

---

## 4. Answer-writing rules for this desk

- First sentence answers the question (Graham; Tan; Basedash).
- Each answer stands alone; reviewers may skip around (Basedash).
- Numbers carry a definition and a date; no "users" alone.
- Name obstacles before the partners do.
- No banned words, no adjectives about quality, no "platform".
- Every fact from `desk/profile.md`; `[TBC]` for the rest.
- Read the whole application aloud; cut every word you can (Graham's red-pen test).
