# YC interview: format, question bank, answer style

Researched October 2026. Deadlines, rules and policies change: check each programme's own page before acting.

Research of 17 October 2026 for a W27 interview (invitations in November and December 2026). Sources at the end.
Company facts only from the applicant's profile file `desk/profile.md`; `[TBC]` for anything only the founders
know.

---

## 1. The format today

From YC's interview guide and Dalton Caldwell's 2023 talk:

- **Ten minutes, on Zoom.** The W27 apply page says most interviews are by video conference in November and
  December. Older accounts of in-person Mountain View interviews (2012–2016) no longer describe the process.
- **All founders on the call.** YC prefers each founder to answer at least one question.
- **Two to three partners** (Caldwell 2023 says two to four). They have the application open and take notes
  while you talk. They are likely the partners who would work with you in the batch.
- **No small talk, no presentation.** YC says it does two things: ask questions and look at what you have built.
- **Questions are basic and depend on your stage.** Pre-launch teams get "when do you launch?"; crowded markets
  get differentiation questions; regulated markets get regulation questions. (Caldwell 2023)
- **Numbers may be checked.** YC's guide says stated numbers may need verification after the call. Keep metrics
  written down in front of you; YC suggests this so you are not reciting from memory.
- **Decision usually the same day.** YC typically decides the day of the interview. Everyone interviewed gets
  detailed feedback. (Apply page; Interview guide)
- **Interviewers run late.** One 2022 founder waited a long time on Zoom because partners were behind schedule.
  (POCIT) Keep the slot and the hour after it clear.

**How partners run it.** The first question is almost always some form of "What is your company working on?"
(Interview guide; Hackernoon international advice). From there the call follows whatever is unclear or
interesting. Caldwell: partners ask themselves whether they can have a productive working relationship with you.
Jessica Livingston: there is no fixed format; do not be thrown if it goes somewhere unexpected.

**What a good interview shows** (Caldwell 2023): the founders know their business cold, including numbers, risks
and next steps; they are credible, honest and self-aware about the hard parts, yet confident; they are
themselves, not on a pitch-contest stage.

## 2. Answer style that works

1. **Answer the question asked, first, in one or two sentences.** Gyfted's alumni advice: aim for 10–15 second
   answers. Hackernoon: answer precisely; adding context to every answer makes you repeat yourself.
2. **Plain words.** Livingston: no buzzwords. Tan: plain English is how YC tests whether you know your stuff.
3. **Specific beats general.** YC's guide: partners prefer interesting details only someone in your field would
   care about to boring generalizations.
4. **Numbers with definitions.** Know users, active users, growth, retention, where users come from, unit
   economics if any. Have them on a sheet.
5. **Say "I don't know" when you don't.** YC's guide: a smart person sincerely working through an unexpected
   question can lead to a good discussion. Caldwell: stretching the truth earns low scores.
6. **Be candid about obstacles.** YC's guide: candid talk about difficulties convinces more than glib dismissal.
   Caldwell: claiming you have it all figured out reads as a lack of self-awareness.
7. **Converse, don't recite.** Caldwell: memorized paragraphs and answers to a question you expected rather than
   the one asked are what partners dislike most.
8. **Every founder speaks.** Agree beforehand who takes product, users and numbers, and who takes engineering.
   Gyfted's alumni: do not jump in while a co-founder is talking; listen and look at them.
9. **Assume they know nothing about Nigeria.** Hackernoon (international founders): assume no knowledge of your
   country, market or local players. Speak slowly and clearly; partners may not be used to your accent.
10. **Don't assume they remember your application.** Repeat the key fact, then add detail. (Hackernoon)

## 3. Question bank (70 questions)

### What you do and the product
1. What is your company working on? [YC]
2. What are you making, in a nutshell? [AJ]
3. How does the product actually work, step by step? [AJ]
4. Can we see a demo? [YC]
5. What is new about what you make? [AJ]
6. Who exactly is the user, and what do they do in the product on day one? [AJ]
7. What is the next thing on the roadmap, and why that? [AJ]
8. Where is the hard part, technically? [AJ]
9. What is the core judgement your product makes for a user, and how do you know it is right? [Expected]
10. How do you keep your data accurate and current? [Expected]

### Users and evidence of need
11. How many users do you have? How many are active? [AJ]
12. How do you define active? [Expected]
13. Where do new users come from? [YC]
14. What makes new users try you? [YC]
15. Why do the reluctant ones hold back? [YC]
16. What are the top things users want? [YC]
17. What has surprised you about user behaviour? [YC]
18. Do users come back? What is retention? [YC; AJ]
19. How do you know people need this? [HM; AJ]
20. Have you had the problem yourself? [AJ]
21. Who is the most desperate user, and how do you reach them first? [AJ]
22. What is one concrete outcome a user got from the product? [Expected]

### Growth and distribution
23. What is your growth rate, weekly or monthly? [AJ]
24. How did you get the users you have? [AJ]
25. How will you get the next 10,000? [DC; AJ]
26. How many users came through your own networks or partner organisations versus on their own? [Expected]
27. What is your conversion from signup to active? [AJ]
28. What one metric shows the company is working? [AJ]
29. What resistance will users have to trying you, and how will you overcome it? [TC; HM]

### Market
30. How big can this get, realistically? [DC; AJ]
31. What do you know about this space that others don't? [AJ; HM]
32. What are the key things about your field that outsiders don't understand? [TC; HM]
33. Why is now the right time? [AJ]
34. If you succeed, what adjacent areas could you expand into? [TC; HM]
35. How would expansion beyond your first country work? [AJ]
36. If your users have little money, who pays? [Expected]

### Competition
37. Who are your competitors? [AJ]
38. Which competitor do you fear most? [AJ]
39. Why hasn't someone already done this, or why did they fail? [AJ]
40. What stops a larger company from copying you? [AJ]
41. Won't LinkedIn or a big job site just add this feature? [AJ]
42. This is a common startup idea. Why is your version different now? [Expected; tarpit check]

### Money
43. How do you make money, or how will you? [DC; AJ]
44. What is your pricing, and why? [AJ]
45. How much revenue could this make per year? [AJ]
46. What does it cost to acquire a user? [AJ]
47. What is your monthly spend and runway? [AJ]
48. Have you raised money? Are you raising now? [AJ]
49. What will you do with YC's money? [AJ]
50. Is a parent company's client revenue part of the startup's revenue? [Expected]

### Team
51. Who does what, day to day? [HM; AJ]
52. How did you meet, and how long have you worked together? [AJ]
53. What have you built together before? [AJ]
54. Who is the boss? How do you make decisions? [AJ]
55. How do you handle disagreements? What was your worst conflict? [AJ]
56. Is everyone full-time? If not, when? [GY; YC FAQ]
57. What happens to your other company or non-profit if you are accepted? [Expected]
58. You live in different countries. How do you work, and where will the company be? [Expected]
59. How did you divide equity? [DC; AJ]
60. Who would you hire next, and why? [LS; AJ]
61. What gaps does the team still have? [AJ]

### Why you, and the founders
62. What's an impressive thing each of you has done? [HM; AJ]
63. What system have you hacked? [AJ]
64. Why are you the right team for this? [AJ]
65. Why should we believe it's you and not someone else? [AJ]

### The idea, risk and the future
66. Six months from now, what will be your biggest problem? [TC; HM]
67. What is the biggest reason you might fail? [AJ]
68. If your main assumption is wrong, what then? [AJ]
69. Are you open to changing the idea? [AJ; POCIT]
70. What other ideas did you consider? Someone just showed us an idea like this and we didn't like it; what else
    do you have? [AJ]
71. If we don't fund you, what will you do? [AJ]
72. What's the biggest mistake you have made so far? [HM; AJ]
73. Where do you see the company in five years? [AJ]

### YC-specific
74. Why YC, and what do you want from it? [AJ]
75. Have you applied before? What changed? [AJ]
76. Can you all be in San Francisco for the batch? Will you reincorporate in the US? [AJ]

## 4. Red flags partners name

- A minute in, they still don't know what you do (Seibel, via Quartz search summary; YC guide).
- Memorized speeches; answering the question you prepared instead of the one asked (Caldwell 2023).
- Stretching the truth on numbers, customers or background (Caldwell 2023).
- Overconfidence: "everything is figured out" (Caldwell 2018).
- Not knowing your own numbers or equity split (Caldwell 2018).
- Treating the call as adversarial or as a stage pitch ("Hello, people, we are here to tell you about...")
  (Caldwell 2023).
- Coaching that makes you deliberately vague about the company, market or revenue (Caldwell 2023).
- A founder who says nothing, or founders talking over each other (YC guide; Gyfted).
- A co-founder whose commitment is unclear (Gyfted).
- Relying on the demo to explain what you do (Livingston).

## 5. How to prepare

**What YC itself says.** Little preparation is needed; heavy rehearsal is often counter-productive, because
friends and even alumni give well-meant advice that backfires. The best edge is progress between application
and interview: launch, ship, grow. (Interview guide) Caldwell (2023): some practice with others can help if it
lowers nerves, but drilled, robotic answers frustrate partners.

**Where others differ.** Justin Kan (via LTSE) argues for many mock interviews with distractions to simulate
pressure. Gyfted's team did mock interviews with YC alumni and found them very helpful. Raushenbush offers mock
interviews to invitees. The desk's reading: rehearse the facts and the first answer, not paragraphs.

**The plan (about ten days between invitation and call).**
1. **Progress sprint.** Pick one metric to move before the interview and move it (YC guide). For example a
   weekly active count or the core action completed `[TBC]`.
2. **The first answer.** Two sentences on what the company does, tested on strangers until they can repeat it back.
   Then the 50-character line as a fallback.
3. **The two-minute version.** For when they say "tell us more": what it does (20 s), who uses it and how many,
   with definitions (30 s), why now (20 s), why us (20 s), what is hard (20 s), next step (10 s). Never deliver it
   unprompted.
4. **The numbers sheet.** One page, in front of both founders: registered accounts, actives and definition,
   weekly and monthly growth with bases, retention, sources of users (affiliated versus organic), the core
   product counts, outcomes, spend, runway, equity split. Every figure dated and exportable.
5. **The demo.** Loaded and logged in, ready to screen-share. A 60–90 second recorded walkthrough as backup
   (YC guide asks for a demo video if the product can't be shown live). Start from a user's point of view and show the
   core action; don't tour menus.
6. **The hard questions, answered in two sentences each:** any other company or non-profit (full-time plan with
   date and names), the founders' locations, visas (see `deal-and-logistics.md`), who pays, why this is not a
   known failed idea, and any metric that looks weak.
7. **Mock interviews: two, not ten.** One with a YC alum if a true contact exists; one with a founder who has
   never seen the product, to test clarity. Use the question bank, cut at ten minutes, record and review.
8. **Logistics.** Both founders on stable connections in quiet rooms (confirm the slot in every founder's time
   zone), cameras at eye level, power backup where the grid is unreliable, a phone hotspot ready, and the same Zoom link
   tested the day before.
9. **Roles.** One founder opens and takes product, users and market; the other takes the build, the stack,
   data and quality. Each takes at least one question.

## 6. What happens after

- **Same day, usually.** YC typically decides on interview day; founders report an email or phone call that
  evening. Do not book anything right after the slot. (Apply page; founder accounts)
- **Sometimes a follow-up.** Onu (2022) received an email about 12 hours after its second interview asking for one
  more call the next morning; the call ended in an offer. Hackernoon notes a second interview with different
  partners when the first pair can't agree. Hoxton Mix (in-person era) was called back the same day.
- **Sometimes "change the idea."** Onu's partners liked the founders but not the idea, held a brainstorm, and
  asked for a new application within a week and a second interview two days later. Be ready to say yes or no to
  that on the call. (POCIT)
- **If not selected:** an email with feedback, usually on the single biggest concern. Teams often fix it,
  reapply next batch and get in. (Interview guide; Caldwell)
- **If accepted:** YC commits the standard deal that day and starts the investment process without waiting for
  the batch; incorporation, visas and the kick-off follow (see `deal-and-logistics.md`).

## 7. African and international founders: what their stories teach

- **Paystack (Nigeria, W16).** First Nigerian company in YC (accepted 11 November 2015). Shola Akinlade applied
  as a solo founder, with 12 pilot merchants, a waiting list of up to 400 and very small revenue; his answers were
  plain and specific (the exact substitute: Nigerian firms setting up UK or US entities to use foreign
  processors). He had applied before without success. Lesson: small, honest numbers and a precise substitute
  are enough to be read. Note: a friend emailed partners on his behalf; YC today says introductions do not
  count, so do not copy that part. (Paystack blog; Chingu recap)
- **Onu (US-based Black founders, 2022 interview).** Partners said they liked the founders and not the idea,
  turned the call into a brainstorm, and invited a resubmission within a week. Lesson: list real alternate ideas
  and be open on the call. (POCIT)
- **Konsus (Norway, W16).** At the first office hours the founders' "succinct" one-line description was cut to a
  few words by Sam Altman. Lesson: shorter than you think. (YC blog, international founder)
- **Hackernoon international advice (2016).** Assume no knowledge of your country; speak slowly; have a crisp
  one-line answer, because a confusing opener can eat the whole interview, "especially if you are an
  international company".
- **Mobile Forms (Nigeria, 2016) and Timon (2026).** Oluwatomi Ayorinde's team nearly missed an in-person YC
  interview because of the visa backlog in Lagos; visas came with days to spare and they were not selected. He
  later got into YC with another company (CrowdForce, W20). Today interviews are by video, so travel only
  matters after acceptance. (TechCabal, October 2026)
- **Seibel in Lagos (2016 meetup; 2021 visit).** He described pattern-matching on the team, said a technical
  co-founder is a must, and said most African applications come from Nigeria. (Chingu; TechCabal 2021)
- **The trend.** Nigeria had 18 of 24 African startups in W22. Since YC returned to in-person batches, African
  acceptances fell: TechCrunch reported none in the summer 2024 batch and three in each of the three batches
  before it; a YC spokesperson tied this to the requirement to move to San Francisco. (TechCrunch 2022 and 2024)
  The open YC company dataset shows only a handful of Africa-based companies after 2023. Lesson: the bar is not
  higher for Africans, but the logistics are; answer the visa and location questions before partners ask.
