# Field guide: accelerators, startup competitions, pitches, credits and innovation funds

Researched October 2026. Deadlines, rules and policies change: check each programme's own page before acting.

Every claim comes from a page listed in section 6. Where two sources disagree, both are given and the conflict is
named. Facts about the company come only from the applicant's profile file `desk/profile.md`.

Use it for any form that asks about a company: accelerators, pitch competitions, demo days, cloud credits, venture
studios, innovation funds and startup awards. Grants for a non-profit are in `grants.md`.

---

## 1. How partners and judges decide

**The order of weight is stable across programmes.** Techstars ranks its criteria in public: team first, then
market, then traction, then the idea. Antler, Microtraction, Ventures Platform and YC say the same thing in
different words: they back people, and ideas change. The idea is the last thing judged, not the first.

**Reviewers read fast.** YC partners have described reading about 100 applications a day in a review window.
DocSend's data puts investor time on a whole deck at under three minutes, and failed decks lose the reader at
about two minutes. A reviewer who cannot say what you do after one sentence moves on.

**The first gate is usually a screen, not a judgment.** MIT Solve staff first check that an entry is complete,
coherent and on-brief; only then do experts score it, and only the top 50 go forward. The Google accelerator
reviewed close to 3,000 applications in one cycle with a short form and a 15–20 minute call. Startupbootcamp took
about 20 teams to Selection Days from roughly 2,000. Fit and completeness end most applications before quality
is weighed.

**Rubrics are explicit; use them.**

| Programme | What is scored |
|---|---|
| MEST AI Startup Program | Three blocks: product and market validation (problem, market and competition, early traction); feasibility (build readiness, money, scale); team (strength, coachability, fit) |
| MIT Solve | Alignment with the challenge; potential for impact; feasibility; a new approach; people-first design. Scalability, partnership potential and technical feasibility count only from the finalist round |
| Hult Prize (chapter scorecard) | Team, idea, impact, business viability, 25% each; impact must have KPIs and grow with revenue |
| Hult Prize (global) | Product-market fit at Nationals; traction (pilots, revenue, customers, partners) in the accelerator; scaling plan at the Finals |
| Lagos Innovates vouchers | Out of 100: market opportunity, team, traction to date, and a credible third-party recommendation. Pass mark 60; the recommendation is optional but lifts the score |
| Orange Corners Nigeria | New approach, feasibility, social impact, market clarity, sustainability and scale, team competence |
| GESAwards (edtech) | A real learning pain point, the teaching approach, user experience, growth potential, a sustainable model |
| University pitch rubrics (Memphis, UWSP, UTA) | Weighted lines for idea, model, market, team, presentation; some score "current status" so that a live venture beats a notion |

**Progress beats promise.** YC calls progress since a previous application a strong signal. One competition rubric
states outright that the teams with the most execution score above the teams with the best ideas. Smartcompany's
list of rejection reasons includes "waiting for the programme to start": accelerators want founders who have
already spoken to customers and built something.

**Momentum is read in weekly or monthly terms.** A small company growing fast is more interesting to YC than a
larger one that is flat. Paul Graham's benchmark during YC is 5–7% growth a week, with 1% a sign the team has not
found its footing; critics (TechCrunch, 2016) argue early growth rates are a weak predictor on their own.

**Venture scale is a separate question from quality.** Kola Aina of Ventures Platform tells founders to check
whether the business can return a venture fund before asking for venture money. Grants, loans and customer
revenue are the right capital for many good businesses. A pitch to a VC-backed accelerator has to show a path to
a large outcome; a pitch to a prize or impact fund has to show impact per unit of money.

**Fit is checked before merit.** VC4A's example: a West African fintech applying to an East African health
programme is out at once. Startupik lists choosing by prestige over stage and sector fit as a leading mistake.

**What makes a reviewer stop reading** (VC4A, Techstars' Jenny Fielding via Tech.co, Startupik, WRI Land
Accelerator, Smartcompany, CodingVC):

1. No idea what the company does after the first lines.
2. Paragraphs of essay prose where a line would do.
3. Answering "none" to the competitor question.
4. Traction given as a raw count with no time frame, no growth rate and no definition.
5. Inflated market sizes or growth claimed on a tiny base.
6. A pilot called "adoption"; a waitlist called "demand".
7. A missing or weak video.
8. A solo founder with no one else committed, or a team of friends with no needed skill.
9. A "nice to have" product, or a model where the mission sits outside the product (profit-share to charity).
10. Answers reused across programmes with no reason why this programme, now.
11. Bluffing in the interview instead of saying "I don't know".
12. Treating the interview as a stage pitch; it is a conversation under pushback.

## 2. Question bank

Each group: the wording forms use, where it was seen, what it tests, and the answer structure the desk uses.
Limits are given where the programme publishes them. Where none is published, draft three lengths (one line,
about 50 words, about 150 words) and cut to the box.

### 2.1 Company

- **Seen as:** "What is your company going to make?" (YC, Techstars); "Tell us who you are and what you're building"
  (Antler); company purpose (DocSend's most-read slide at pre-seed); "Describe your solution in one sentence"
  (Solve, competition forms).
- **Tests:** whether a stranger can repeat what you do. DocSend found the purpose line is used as a quick filter
  for thesis fit.
- **Structure:** [what it is] for [who] that [does what], so that [result]. One sentence. No adjectives about
  quality. A comparison to a known product is allowed if it is exact. Then one line of proof (a date, a number).
- **Also asked:** founding year, incorporation details, shareholders and percentages, URL, industries. Keep the company
  registration facts to hand; Techstars and YC both ask for the cap table split.

### 2.2 Problem

- **Seen as:** "What problem is your company looking to solve?" (Techstars); "Why did you pick this idea?" and
  "How do you know people need what you're making?" (YC); "a real challenge for your home city, country or Africa"
  (Google accelerator, earlier rules); problem clarity (MEST).
- **Tests:** whether the founder met the problem personally or found it in a report. Startup Fortune's reading of
  YC: a generic "the market is large" reply signals an outsider's view.
- **Structure:** the moment (who missed what, when) → how often it happens (one sourced figure) → what people do
  today and why it fails → the cost of the failure. Four sentences. The founder story goes here only if the founder
  was the user.

### 2.3 Product

- **Seen as:** "What's new about what you're making?" (YC); product demo video (Techstars); "working prototype with
  pilot results" (UNICEF); functional MVP with demonstrable user traction for software (Africa Prize); "a minimum
  viable product and/or service in the market" (GSA Africa).
- **Tests:** whether it exists and works, and what is different about how it works.
- **Structure:** what the user does in three steps → the one thing it does that substitutes do not → what is live
  now (web, Android, dates) vs what is planned (labelled as planned) → the demo link. Never describe planned
  features in the present tense.

### 2.4 Traction

- **Seen as:** "How far along are you?"; "How many active users or customers do you have?"; monthly growth (YC);
  "Share any traction you have" and "Share any revenue and funding" (Techstars); early traction (MEST); at least ten
  paying customers or three months of active-user data (Lagos Pitchathon gate); monthly recurring revenue (GSA
  Africa); three years of revenue (Africa's Business Heroes).
- **Tests:** evidence that people want it, and how fast that evidence is growing. Revenue is not required at
  pre-seed (Techstars, Microtraction, VC4A), but some proof is.
- **Structure:** the headline count with its definition and date → the active share → growth rate over a named
  period with the starting base → one retention or repeat-use figure → the strongest qualitative proof (a user
  outcome, a partner, a pilot). See section 3 for the rules.

### 2.5 Market

- **Seen as:** "Who is your target audience?" (Techstars); market size and competition (MEST); "significant total
  addressable market" (Google accelerator); market clarity (Orange Corners); market opportunity (Lagos Innovates).
- **Tests:** whether the market exists now and is growing, and whether the founder knows the first customer.
  Startup Fortune notes YC discounts polished TAM slides.
- **Structure:** first customer, described exactly → how many of them exist (bottom-up: count × price or count ×
  usage) → the signal that the market is growing → the next segment. Top-down TAM in one line at most, sourced.

### 2.6 Competition

- **Seen as:** "Who are your competitors or could become your competitors?" and "What is your competitive
  advantage?" (Techstars); "Who are your competitors, and who might become competitors? Who do you fear most?" and
  "What substitutes do people resort to?" (YC, older form).
- **Tests:** market knowledge and honesty. "None" is a red flag (Fielding). DocSend found successful decks were more
  likely to include a competition section, and pre-seed investors spent 51% more time on it in 2020 than in 2019.
- **Structure:** substitutes people use today (including doing nothing) → named direct competitors → the one you
  respect most and why → your edge stated as a fact you can prove (a channel, data, cost, speed), not "better
  design".

### 2.7 Business model

- **Seen as:** "How do you (or will you) make money? How much could you make?" (Techstars, YC); monetisation
  potential (MEST); a capital-efficient model with a revenue path (Microtraction); unit economics and a path to
  profit (Hult); CAC and lifetime value (Lagos Pitchathon judges).
- **Tests:** whether there is a buyer and a price, and whether the economics can work at scale. DocSend: at seed,
  the business model slide drew the most time.
- **Structure:** who pays → for what → at what price (or price being tested) → what it costs to win one payer →
  what revenue looks like at a named scale. If pre-revenue, say so and name the experiment and its date.

### 2.8 Team

- **Seen as:** "What makes your team a winning team?" (Techstars); "most impressive thing each founder has built or
  achieved"; "a time you hacked a non-computer system"; "how long have the founders known each other"; "who
  writes code" (YC); "Are you working on your startup full time?" (Techstars founder profile); team strength and
  coachability (MEST); a technical founder (Microtraction, Google accelerator: CTO must attend).
- **Tests:** can these people build and sell this, how long they have worked together, who does what, and
  full-time commitment. Paul Graham: name one or two specific achievements; avoid "dedicated" and "hard-working".
- **Structure per founder:** role → one hard thing done, with a result → how it bears on this company. Then: years
  worked together, who builds the product in-house, time commitment stated honestly, the gap and how it is being
  filled. Techstars' guide also suggests naming one failed experiment from the last 90 days and the lesson.

### 2.9 Funding and the ask

- **Seen as:** "Share any revenue and funding you have" (Techstars); funding status used to judge maturity
  (NVIDIA); "Are you fundraising?" (YC); raised under $1M (AFTS, Web Summit ALPHA); under €10M and founded 2023 or
  later (Slush 100, third-party listing); institutional equity required for top credit tiers (Google Cloud Scale,
  AWS Portfolio).
- **Tests:** stage fit and honesty. Many gates are funding ceilings or floors.
- **Structure:** total raised to date by type (grants, prizes, equity, revenue), with funders named → what is open
  now → the ask: amount, what it buys, the milestone it reaches, by when. Grants and prizes are not equity; say
  which is which.

### 2.10 Why now

- **Seen as:** "Why is now the right time for your startup?" (Techstars); market timing (Techstars blog); DocSend's
  "why now" slide (read more at pre-seed than at Series A).
- **Tests:** a change that makes this possible or urgent today.
- **Structure:** one concrete, recent change (a cost drop, a new channel, a behaviour shift, a policy) → what it
  enables → why waiting loses the window. Startup Fortune: sweeping narratives carry little weight; specific recent
  changes do.

### 2.11 Why us, why this programme

- **Seen as:** "Why you?" (YC, DocSend's founder slide); people-first design: the team has experience and
  relationships with the community (Solve); "How would you benefit from Solve's network?" (Solve partnership
  potential); programme selection with reasons (Techstars).
- **Tests:** an unfair advantage, and a specific bottleneck the programme fixes. Startupik: tie the programme to the
  bottleneck, not to prestige.
- **Structure:** the advantage (lived experience, a channel, data, a skill) as a fact → the bottleneck now → which
  part of this programme removes it (a named mentor track, credit, market, partner) → what success looks like at
  the end of the programme.

### 2.12 Milestones and use of funds

- **Seen as:** "What are your next major company milestones?" (Techstars); a pilot plan (Mastercard EdTech
  Fellowship); a plan to reach scale (Hult finals).
- **Structure:** three milestones, each a number and a date ("X active users by 31 March 2027"); the spend that
  reaches each; the assumption each one tests.

### 2.13 Video

- **Seen as:** the YC founder video; Techstars' team introduction and product demo videos; founder introduction
  video (Africa's Business Heroes); video submission (MEST); pitch deck plus video (GITEX Supernova); two-minute
  video pitches (university and challenge competitions). See section 4.
- **Tests:** how the founders talk, and how they work together.
- **Structure (one minute):** names and roles (10 s) → what we make, for whom (15 s) → why us, one fact (15 s) →
  the proof so far (10 s) → what we want next (10 s). Spoken from bullet points, not read.

### 2.14 Curveballs

- **Seen as:** "Something surprising you have discovered" and "other ideas you considered" (YC); "something you
  hacked" (YC); assessment tests (TEF business test; MEST assessment, required before review).
- **Structure:** a true, specific anecdote with a result; no inflation. Partners sometimes fund the alternate idea,
  so list real ones.

## 3. Metrics and honesty: reporting early traction without inflation

**The rules the desk follows.**

1. **Name the count.** "Registered accounts" is cumulative and only rises. "Active users" needs a definition and a
   window. Never write "users" alone in an application.
2. **Date every figure** and keep the export it came from. "1,200 registered accounts as of [date]" survives due
   diligence; "1,200+ users" does not.
3. **Give actives beside totals.** Investors read the ratio. Hustle Fund and others list registered users, downloads
   and raw page views as vanity counts; the useful figures are actives, retention, conversion and repeat use.
4. **Define "active" by the core action,** not by a login. For a matching product: opened at least one match, saved
   one, or set a reminder, in the window.
5. **Growth always with its base.** "Up 400%" from what? Give the start and end counts and the period. Weekly or
   monthly, not "since launch".
6. **Separate the channels.** Users brought in by your own non-profit, events or launch push are real but
   affiliated. Hustle Fund warns that early numbers built on the founder's network may not repeat. Say how many came
   unprompted.
7. **Launch spikes are not traction.** Signup-to-active conversion after a launch is often in single digits. Show
   what happened in the months after.
8. **Words for weak signals stay weak.** A pilot is a pilot. A waitlist is a list. An LOI is intent. Startupik and
   Hustle Fund both flag the upgrade of these words as an overstatement reviewers spot.
9. **Exclude bots and staff,** and say so in a footnote.
10. **Small and honest beats large and vague.** VC4A: founders with little traction can show the steps they are
    taking to build it. Codingvc: exaggerated traction on a tiny base is a top demo-day mistake.
11. **Rules of thumb are for reading, not reporting.** Fred Wilson's 30/10/10 heuristic estimates how many registered
    users are likely active. Use it to sanity-check your own data, never in place of it.

**Benchmarks found (use with care).**

| Benchmark | Source | Reliability |
|---|---|---|
| 5–7% weekly growth is good during YC; 10% exceptional; 1% weak | Paul Graham, "Startup = Growth" (via secondary quotes) | Widely cited; contested as a predictor |
| 20% month-on-month cited as a Techstars benchmark | causo.ai guide | Not confirmed on Techstars' own pages |
| Ten paying customers or three months of active-user data | Lagos Pitchathon 2026 entry rule | Event-specific |
| MVP plus monthly recurring revenue, team of 2+ | GSA Africa 2024 rules | Programme rule |
| Three years of revenue | Africa's Business Heroes 2026 | Programme rule |
| MVP, a few users, key hypotheses tested | Ventures Platform entry point | Investor statement |

**Answer pattern for the traction box.**

> [N] registered accounts since [launch date] ([source], exported [date]). [M] were active in the last 30 days
> (active = [core action]). Accounts grew from [a] to [b] between [month] and [month] ([x]% a month). [R]% of
> users who joined in [month] were still active 30 days later. [One outcome: a user who won a place through a
> match, with consent.]

Every bracket stays `[TBC]` until the applicant supplies the export.

## 4. Decks, videos and demos

### Decks

- **Three tests per slide** (Kevin Hale, YC): legible from the back row, one idea per slide, the point stated in the
  headline so no one has to work it out. A graph gets a caption that says what it shows.
- **Length.** Hale works from the five to seven points you want remembered, and a short deck. DocSend says most
  decks run about 20 pages at about 50 words a slide. For a written application, send the shorter deck.
- **Order.** DocSend found successful pre-seed decks put product and "why now" early and had no table of contents.
  The team slide appears in every deck; financials appeared in none of the failed ones it studied.
- **Time.** Under three minutes per deck on average (2022). Successful pre-seed decks in 2020 held readers for over
  four minutes; unsuccessful ones about 90 seconds.
- **Screenshots.** Hale prefers a short list of steps to an interface screenshot; screenshots are slow to read.
- **Remove:** dense text, animations, memes, jokes, extra branding, typos (codingvc).
- **Files:** Antler's pitch upload is capped at 10MB; NVIDIA and Antler both take a deck at application.
- **Slide set for pre-seed applications:** purpose line → problem → product (steps, not screens) → traction (dated,
  defined) → why now → market (bottom-up) → competition (substitutes plus named players) → model → team → ask and
  milestones. Ten to twelve slides.

### Videos

- **YC founder video:** about one minute; every founder on camera (a recorded video call is fine); nothing but the
  founders talking; no demo, music or effects; bullet points, not a script. YC says applicants who submit one are
  more likely to be interviewed. Upload unlisted, not private; check the audio (Startup Grind notes many are
  inaudible).
- **Partners watch the founder relationship.** A Pilot founder's advice: be at ease with each other and do not talk
  over one another. No gimmicks.
- **Techstars:** asks for a team introduction video and a separate product demo video. Fielding: an application
  without a video of product and founders is hard to evaluate.
- **Competition videos:** often two minutes, sometimes two and a half at most; the applicant on camera; no slideshow
  with voice-over as the pitch; make the proposition clear in the first 30 seconds; production quality is not
  scored; the link must open without a password (UC Davis, Longitude Explorer and GWP guidance via search).
- **African programmes:** Africa's Business Heroes asks for a founder introduction video; MEST asks for a video at
  the general application stage; GITEX Supernova asks for a deck and a video through the exhibitor portal.
- **Standard for the desk:** film on a phone in daylight, one take per founder, under 60 seconds unless the form says
  otherwise, captions burnt in for silent viewing, loudness normalised.

### Demos

- **Live demos fail on stage.** Use a recorded walkthrough or a scripted local build; keep a backup video; offer a
  one-to-one demo afterwards (codingvc, YourStory, Built In).
- **Start from the user's problem,** then show the product solving it. Do not tour every button.
- **Interviews are demo-led conversations,** not pitches (YC founders via Startup Grind; Smartcompany; Startupik).
  Know the demo cold.
- **Some events require a live walkthrough.** The Lagos Pitchathon format was a five-minute product walkthrough and a
  three-minute question round, with judges probing CAC and lifetime value and the audience holding a quarter of the
  vote.
